How real companies decide who gets which promotion

Retailers and delivery apps no longer send the same discount to everyone. They use what customers buy, when they shop and how they react to past offers to decide who gets a promotion, which one, and when. This page explains how that works and links to first-hand sources from companies that do it.

Why companies target promotions

A blanket discount is expensive. Much of the money goes to customers who would have bought anyway, so the company gives away margin without gaining any sales. Some customers also learn to wait for discounts, which trains them to stop paying full price.

Targeting tries to spend the promotion budget only where it changes behaviour: bringing back a lapsing customer, getting someone to try a new category, or nudging a basket over a spend threshold. The goal is incremental sales, meaning sales that would not have happened without the offer.

To do this, companies need two things: detailed behaviour data (usually from a loyalty card or app login) and a way to predict how each customer will respond to each possible offer.

How it works end to end

The details differ by company, but most systems in this guide follow the same sequence.

  1. Collect behaviour data

    Every transaction is tied to a customer through a loyalty card, app account or login. This gives purchase history, visit frequency, basket size, preferred categories, channel and time of day, plus how the customer responded to past offers.

  2. Turn it into features

    Raw transactions become signals a model can use: days since last purchase, spend trend, share of spend on promotion, category affinity, price sensitivity, likely next visit date.

  3. Predict the response to each offer

    A model estimates, for each customer and each candidate offer, how likely they are to redeem it and, ideally, how much extra they will buy because of it.

  4. Choose offers within a budget

    An optimiser or bandit picks the best offer for each customer while keeping total discount cost under the campaign budget and respecting business rules (one offer per category, brand-funded offers, and so on).

  5. Deliver at the right moment

    Offers go out by app, email, mailer or receipt, often timed to when the customer is expected to shop next.

  6. Measure against a control group

    A random holdout group gets no offer (or a standard one). Comparing the two groups shows the true incremental effect, and those results train the next round of models.

The three methods you will see

Almost every case below combines these building blocks. Older programmes also rely on simpler customer segmentation and redemption-probability scoring, which are covered in the grocery cases.

1. Uplift modelling

Who buys because of the promotion?

A normal model predicts whether a customer will buy. An uplift model predicts the difference the offer makes: the chance they buy with the offer minus the chance they buy without it. It is trained on past experiments where some customers randomly got an offer and others did not.

This splits customers into four groups:

PersuadablesBuy only if they get the offer. This is where the budget should go.
Sure thingsBuy anyway. An offer here is wasted margin.
Lost causesWill not buy either way. An offer here is wasted too.
Sleeping dogsBuy less if contacted, for example by being reminded to cancel. Leave them alone.
Example: two customers each have a 60% chance of ordering this week. For one, a ₹100 discount raises it to 62%; for the other, to 85%. A response model treats them the same. An uplift model gives the discount only to the second.

2. Budget optimisation

Who gets which offer, within budget?

Once you know the expected lift and cost of every customer–offer pair, you still have to choose. Each customer can get at most one offer, and total spend has a limit. This is a classic knapsack problem: fill a bag of fixed size with the items worth the most. Companies solve it with linear programming or constraint solvers, often with a pacer that spreads spend evenly across a campaign.

Example: with a ₹10 lakh budget, giving 20% off to 5,000 high-uplift customers may beat 10% off to 10,000 medium-uplift customers. The optimiser compares these options across millions of customers at once.

3. Contextual bandits

Which offer works for this person, right now?

Uplift models need historical experiments. A bandit learns while running: it mostly shows the offer it currently thinks is best for a customer's context, but sometimes tries alternatives to keep learning. Over time it shifts towards offers that get redeemed. "Contextual" means it takes the customer's features into account instead of finding one best offer for everyone.

Example: a "spend $50, get $10" offer works for frequent shoppers, while "spend $30, get $5" works for occasional ones. The bandit discovers this split by testing, without anyone defining the segments in advance.

Engineering blogs and papers

Written by the teams that built the systems. These are the best sources for understanding the methods in practice.

DoorDash
Food delivery, US
uplift · causal ML
Problem
Uniform discounts (the same amount for everyone) cost a lot and give much of the money to customers who would order anyway.
Data
Order history and past promotion experiments, where users randomly received different offers.
How it works
Uses Double Machine Learning, a causal inference technique, to estimate how much each user's likelihood of ordering would change under each specific offer. Discount amounts are then personalised per user based on that estimated lift.
Result
Higher order-rate lift while improving cost efficiency compared with uniform discount amounts.
Takeaway
A clear real-world example of uplift modelling replacing "who is likely to order" with "who orders more because of the discount".

Uber / Uber Eats
Rides & food delivery, global
uplift + knapsack
Problem
Many incentive types (rider promos, Eats discounts, driver incentives) compete for one budget, and each user can receive only a limited number.
Data
Trip and order history, plus results from past incentive experiments.
How it works
Uplift models forecast the incremental effect of each incentive on metrics such as Eats orders and trips. The system then treats allocation as a Multiple Knapsack Problem and uses a constraint solver (CP-SAT) to pick incentives, with a budget pacer controlling spend over time.
Takeaway
Shows the second half of the pipeline: prediction alone is not enough, you also need an optimiser to turn predictions into decisions under a budget.

Target
General retail, US
contextual bandit
Problem
Target Circle members get "spend X, get Y" bonus offers. Set the threshold too high and nobody redeems; too low and Target pays for spend that would have happened anyway.
Data
Transactions, past promotions and guest behaviour from the Target Circle loyalty programme.
How it works
CORE (Contextual Offer Recommendations Engine) is a contextual multi-armed bandit that chooses the spend threshold and reward amount for each member, optimising for offer adds and redemptions. A separate deep learning model, TargetRun, predicts when each guest will shop next so offers are timed for that trip.
Takeaway
Personalisation is not only about which product: the offer's size, threshold and timing are tuned per person too.

Booking.com
Online travel, global
uplift + knapsack
Problem
Run promotions that pay for themselves: the extra bookings must cover the cost of the discounts.
Data
Booking behaviour from large randomised promotion experiments.
How it works
Uplift models estimate each customer's incremental response to each promotion. An online multiple-choice knapsack algorithm then allocates promotions in real time while keeping spend within the budget.
Result
+162% sales while meeting a zero-net-budget constraint, according to their published paper.
Takeaway
With uplift and budget control together, promotions can become self-funding long-running campaigns rather than one-off costs.

Grocery loyalty programmes

Grocery is where personalised couponing started, because shoppers visit weekly and loyalty cards link every basket to a household. These sources read best as business case studies.

Kroger / 84.51°
Grocery, US
propensity scoring
Background
84.51° is Kroger's in-house data science company, built from dunnhumby's former US operations. It is named after the longitude of Kroger's Cincinnati headquarters.
Data
First-party loyalty data from over 60 million US households and around 2 billion transactions a year.
How it works
For every eligible household, the system computes the probability of redeeming each of roughly 2,000 possible offers. The best combination goes into personalised mailers and digital-only coupons. Models also detect when a customer is likely to stock up on a product.
Result
Some targeted coupons reach ~65% redemption against a national average of about 5%.
Takeaway
Matching offers to products a household already buys or is about to need gives very high redemption. Kroger also sells this targeting to brands, which fund many of the offers.

Tesco Clubcard / dunnhumby
Grocery, UK
segmentation
Background
Clubcard launched in 1995 with analytics from dunnhumby and is often cited as the programme that showed loyalty data could drive promotions at scale.
Data
Basket-level shopping history from more than 23 million UK households.
How it works
Early on, customers were grouped into detailed segments, and each segment received different vouchers. By 1997 this had become close to one-to-one couponing. Today Tesco issues individual digital coupons and personalised spending challenges designed to get members to try new products, spend more in a category or raise their overall spend.
Result
289M personalised coupons to about 8M customers in one year.
Takeaway
The same data also drives pricing, range and category decisions, so the loyalty programme becomes a strategic data asset, not just a discount scheme.

App-based rewards

Starbucks Deep Brew
Coffee chain, global
recommender + RL
Problem
Personalise offers for tens of millions of Rewards members while keeping offers relevant to what each store can actually serve.
Data
Order history from the Starbucks app and Rewards programme, combined with context such as time of day, weather and local store inventory.
How it works
Deep Brew combines collaborative filtering (people like you ordered this), content-based filtering (similar to what you order) and reinforcement learning to choose personalised offers and suggestions, such as challenges to visit more often or try a new item.
Takeaway
A feedback loop: more app use gives more data, which gives better offers, which drives more app use. Context (weather, inventory) matters as much as the customer's history.

Risks and criticism

Privacy. The same loyalty data used for coupons is often sold or shared with brands and advertisers. Customers may not realise how much their shopping history reveals or where it goes.

Fairness. Personalised offers mean different customers effectively pay different prices for the same product. Regulators and the press increasingly ask whether this disadvantages certain groups.

Discount dependency. Poorly targeted promotions train customers to wait for offers, which lowers full-price sales over time. Uplift modelling and control groups exist partly to catch this.

Glossary

Incremental sales (lift)
Sales that happened because of the promotion, measured against a group that did not receive it.
Control / holdout group
A random set of customers deliberately left out of a campaign so its true effect can be measured.
Propensity model
Predicts how likely a customer is to do something, such as redeem a coupon. It does not say whether the coupon caused it.
Uplift model
Predicts the change in behaviour caused by an action, by comparing treated and untreated customers.
Double Machine Learning (DML)
A causal inference method that uses machine learning models to remove the effect of other factors and isolate the effect of the treatment, here the discount.
Knapsack problem
An optimisation problem: choose items with the highest total value without exceeding a fixed capacity, here the promotion budget.
Multi-armed bandit
An algorithm that balances trying new options (exploration) with using the best-known option (exploitation). Contextual bandits also use customer features.
Redemption rate
The share of issued offers that customers actually use.
First-party data
Data a company collects directly from its own customers, such as loyalty card transactions, rather than buying it from others.
