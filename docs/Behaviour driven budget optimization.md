# Budget optimization funds persuadable customers, not likely buyers

Industry uses customer behaviour prediction to find the customers whose behaviour an offer actually changes, then spends a fixed budget where each extra rupee returns the most. **(1) Who and how much:** leading platforms (Uber, DoorDash, Booking.com, Meituan) run a two-stage pipeline. A model estimates the *incremental* effect and incremental cost of each offer for each customer, and an optimizer allocates the budget, usually as a knapsack solved by ROI ranking or a Lagrangian "price of a budget rupee"; channel-level budgets instead use saturating response curves and equalise marginal ROI, as marketing-mix tools like Google Meridian and Meta Robyn do. Plain propensity ("likely to buy") scores are repeatedly shown to waste money on people who would have bought anyway. **(2) Granularity:** it is neither SKU-only nor category-only. The best primary evidence (PepsiCo, 2026) estimates response at SKU level, pools it up a product hierarchy, and makes decisions at an intermediate "product group" level, per retailer, inside trade-spend limits. **(3) Elasticity:** yes, it is used, but mostly for shelf price, promotion depth and calendars, and usually embedded in a machine-learning demand curve rather than a single coefficient; customer-level targeting uses the individual-level equivalent (response to each discount depth). **(4) For the demo:** a simulation with 3 to 5 customer groups, a diminishing-returns curve per group, a water-filling optimizer and a "spend only while each rupee earns back a rupee" switch is faithful to the real methods and fits in about 15 lines of JavaScript. The evidence is strongest for Questions 1 and 3, weaker for Question 2 (nothing primary on named retailers) and absent for Indian retailers; nearly all reported business lifts are self-reported by the companies.

## Predicting who changes behaviour is the core of "who gets an offer"

The central idea is simple enough to explain to a business manager: an offer only creates value for customers it *changes*. Radcliffe and Surry's case studies show why a plain "likely to respond" model fails: in a US Bank direct-mail test, targeting the top 30% by a conventional response model produced **almost exactly zero incremental sales and negative ROI**, whereas the uplift model's different 30% captured about **90% of the incremental sales** available from mailing everyone ([Radcliffe & Surry 2011](https://stochasticsolutions.com/pdf/sig-based-up-trees.pdf)). These are the authors' own consultancy cases, so treat the magnitudes as illustrative rather than typical. The standard way to explain it is four customer types: persuadables (buy only if offered), sure things (buy anyway), lost causes (never buy) and sleeping dogs (buy less if contacted). Only persuadables create value, and DoorDash uses the same logic under the labels "persuadables" and "always buyers" ([DoorDash](https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/doordash-smarter-promotions-with-causal-machine-learning)). The four-box naming itself rests on practitioner and encyclopedic sources ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uplift_modelling); [Elder Research](https://www.elderresearch.com/blog/uplift-modeling-making-predictive-models-actionable/)), not a peer-reviewed definition.

The reported industrial results cluster around cost efficiency at similar impact. DoorDash's targeted re-engagement campaigns delivered about the same incremental order lift as uniform coupons at **roughly half the cost per incremental order**, and personalising discount depth beat a uniform discount in a large A/B test, though it published no exact figures ([DoorDash](https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/doordash-smarter-promotions-with-causal-machine-learning)). Meituan reports **+14.3% incremental orders and +13.6% GMV** over a baseline method in a four-week test across about 310,000 shops ([Zhou et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15728)); Meituan Bike reports +2.8% subscription revenue per exposed user ([Dai et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.05987v3)). Uber's cost-aware learners beat cost-ignorant ones on a real promotion experiment, because a model that ignores what the offer costs cannot find the best net value ([Zhao & Harinen](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.05372)). None of these has independent replication. A useful caution from Booking.com's review of about 150 production models is that better model accuracy does not automatically produce business value, which is why value is measured with randomized controlled trials ([Morning Paper summary](https://blog.acolyer.org/2019/10/07/150-successful-machine-learning-models/)).

Customer lifetime value (CLV) is the weakest-sourced part of the brief. No primary source in the research documents CLV feeding a promotion-budget optimizer. The logical role, an inference, is as the *value* multiplier on an incremental retention or purchase effect, so a persuadable who is worth more over time earns a higher return on the same offer. Next-best-offer systems are best read as the multi-offer version of the same problem: Meituan Bike predicts conversion at each coupon value from a ladder and picks the best affordable one ([Dai et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.05987v3)), while Starbucks and Southeastern Grocers (with dunnhumby) describe personalised offers in company or trade coverage with no published results ([Microsoft Source](https://news.microsoft.com/source/features/digital-transformation/starbucks-turns-to-technology-to-brew-up-a-more-personal-connection-with-its-customers/); [Supermarket News](https://www.supermarketnews.com/grocery-marketing/southeastern-grocers-eyes-more-effective-promotions)).

## The budget is solved as a knapsack for customers and as response curves for channels

Once each customer-offer pair has a predicted incremental value and cost, the allocation is a knapsack problem: pick the pairs that maximise total incremental value without exceeding the budget. For a single offer, the greedy rule is to sort customers by incremental ROI (incremental revenue divided by incremental cost) and treat down the list until the money runs out. With several offer levels per customer (one offer each), it becomes a multiple-choice knapsack, solved by a Lagrangian method: find the shadow price alpha of a budget rupee and give each customer the offer maximising value minus alpha times cost ([Zhou et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.15728)). Booking.com treats eligibility, offer choice and reward thresholds as knapsack variants, reporting an optimality gap of 0.245% against an exact integer program and 51% better results than simple greedy on a real discount dataset ([Goldenberg et al.](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-2959/paper2.pdf)). Scale forces practical solvers: Uber found a linear-programming solver took over 24 hours on about 100,000 users with one lever, while Google OR-Tools CP-SAT solved it in minutes and was stress-tested to millions of users; a feedback "Budget Pacer" then reconciles budget with observed spend ([Uber Engineering](https://uber.com/us/en/blog/solving-multiple-knapsack)). Meituan Bike updates its multiplier in real time with a PID controller to keep spend on budget ([Dai et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.05987v3)).

At the aggregate level (channels, cities, weeks), the formulation is different: each lever has a concave, saturating response curve, and the optimizer moves money until marginal returns are equal across levers. Uber's weekly city-level system builds shape-constrained response surfaces (non-negative, diminishing returns) from a causal deep-learning model and solves with ADMM and convex solvers, though the paper gave no numeric deployment results ([Chen et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.19078)). Google Meridian's default mode maximises ROI for a fixed budget, with per-channel bounds reported at plus or minus 30% of the current allocation, and a flexible mode that keeps spending until marginal ROI falls to a target ([Meridian docs](https://developers.google.com/meridian/docs/user-guide/fixed-budget-maximize-roi)). Robyn offers equivalent "max response" and "target efficiency" scenarios over Hill-saturation curves ([Robyn analyst's guide](https://facebookexperimental.github.io/Robyn/docs/analysts-guide-to-MMM/)). The "equal marginal return at the optimum" property is standard calculus that the research team stated from general knowledge, not a quote from these sources.

Bandits and reinforcement learning are the thinnest category. The only retrieved bandit pipeline with real documentation (Instacart) optimises search ranking, not promotions ([Instacart](https://company.instacart.com/tech-innovation/using-contextual-bandit-models-in-large-action-spaces-at-instacart)), and Starbucks is described only as using reinforcement learning without technical detail. The honest statement for a manager is: bandits exist for sequential, explore-as-you-go decisions, but no primary source here shows them running a promotion budget with measured results. Meituan also argues that "predict, then optimize" accumulates error and trains models to output the decision quantity (ROI or marginal utility) directly ([Zhou et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15728)); Uber's and DoorDash's recent blogs still keep prediction and optimization separate, so both architectures are in use.

## Decisions sit at product-group level, with SKU data underneath and the budget set above

The strongest evidence on granularity is PepsiCo's PromoAI, deployed in all its global markets with 16 calendar refreshes a year. It chooses one promotion option per Product Promotion Group (PPG, a set of similar SKUs) per week for a given retailer's calendar, with 6 to 90+ PPGs per market, and treats trade-spend and margin floors as **constraints on the optimizer**, not outputs; about **85%** of recommendations were accepted and executed in most markets ([Llenas et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.17941)). Its pricing system estimates elasticities at SKU-banner-week level and then aggregates up, so estimation granularity (SKU) differs from decision granularity (group) ([Llenas et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.17941)). SAP's trade-management product shows the same hierarchy mechanically: category-level promotions were added in 2021, and spend and volume are split down to SKUs in proportion to a base volume ratio, while baseline volume rolls up from SKUs ([SAP Community](https://blogs.sap.com/2021/04/05/sap-trade-management-fp05-product-category-planning-new-release/)). Academic work explains why: elasticity counts grow quadratically with product count, and Bayesian priors that shrink SKU estimates toward their brand, subcategory and category group improved predictions by 4.5% overall and 6.8% for products with little price variation ([Smith & Griffin 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11129-022-09260-7)). The practical rule, an inference consistent with these sources, is "estimate low, pool up, decide in between".

Three limits matter for how confidently this is told. First, no primary source documents the full cascade (annual budget by customer, then category, brand, SKU, event) at P&G, Unilever, Nestle, Coca-Cola or any named retailer; that cascade is the research team's inference from tool design. Second, evidence on retailers versus manufacturers is vendor marketing at best: Revionics, Oracle and Kinaxis describe item- and offer-level optimization within a category ([Revionics](https://www.revionics.com/ai-powered-promotions/); [Kinaxis/Rubikloud](https://www.kinaxis.com/sites/default/files/2020-08/Promotion_Forecasting_Optimization_Grocery_Rubikloud.pdf)), and nothing primary was found for Walmart, Kroger, Tesco, Reliance Retail, DMart or BigBasket. The manufacturer-versus-retailer split (manufacturer sets customer then product-group budgets; retailer sets category plans then item offers) is inferred from who owns which profit and loss. Third, for customer-level personalised offers, the pattern is probably a product- or supplier-funded pool that personalisation allocates across segments, but the sources show personalisation exists, not the budget mechanics. No source compared category-only models with SKU-level models head to head.

## Price elasticity is used, but as a learned response curve and alongside customer-level models

Elasticity is a real input at the leading edge, though not always as a single number. PepsiCo's promotion system approximates a LightGBM demand forecast with piecewise-linear segments and embeds it in a mixed-integer program solved with Gurobi, while its pricing system uses a Bayesian hierarchical elasticity model with own-price elasticities expected to fall between **-2.5 and 0** as a sanity gate ([Llenas et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.17941)). Zalando's 2026 pricing system feeds the future discount into a demand forecaster with a monotonicity constraint (a deeper discount can never lower predicted demand), optimises each article separately, and reports across 23 A/B tests a **+6.00% profit contribution** (95% interval 0.79% to 11.03%) while sales gains were not statistically significant ([Birr et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13741)). So "elasticity" in production is often the slope of a machine-learned, shape-constrained response curve. Adoption is not universal: McKinsey reports that nearly half of large European CPG manufacturers have no trade-promotion optimization tool, a consultancy claim ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/how-precision-revenue-growth-management-transforms-cpg-promotions)).

Elasticity and customer-behaviour models answer different questions and combine through the response curve. Elasticity-style demand models tell you how volume responds to price or depth at product-store-week level and suit shelf price and calendar planning where no individual identifiers exist; uplift models tell you which customers respond to a targeted coupon and suit CRM budgets. DoorDash estimating how deep a discount each user needs, and Meituan's marginal utility between adjacent discount levels, are individual-level versions of an elasticity curve ([DoorDash](https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/doordash-smarter-promotions-with-causal-machine-learning); [Zhou et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15728)). That the two combine into one pipeline (customer-group response curves feeding a constrained optimizer) is the research team's inference; no retrieved source shows it end to end at a named firm.

The raw promotional bump is a poor measure of value, which is why "net incremental" adjustments appear everywhere. In CVS's 2003 promotions, about 45% of gross lift was truly incremental, about 45% switched from other items in the store and about 10% was pulled forward from future purchases; **more than half of promotions were unprofitable**, mostly because promotional margin was below regular margin ([Ailawadi et al. 2006](https://faculty.tuck.dartmouth.edu/images/uploads/faculty/kusum-ailawadi/Retail_promotion_profitability_JMR_2006.pdf)). Other studies split the bump roughly into thirds (brand switching, timing, category expansion) at store level ([Van Heerde et al. 2004](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v23y2004i3p317-334.html)) or put about 75% on brand switching at household level ([Bell et al. 1999](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v18y1999i4p504-526.html)); the gap is partly definitional, so any "x% is incremental" figure must state its perspective. Typical magnitudes are a mean own-price elasticity of about **-2.6** across 1,851 estimates ([Bijmolt et al. 2005](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmkr.42.2.141.62296)), with promotional elasticities larger in magnitude than regular-price ones (about -2.3 versus -0.5 to -0.8 in one hierarchical study; [Fok et al. 2005](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/6908/ERS%202005%20047%20MKT.pdf)). Observational estimates are also fragile: ignoring that managers set prices knowing demand shocks biases results, and correcting for it makes elasticities larger in magnitude, so firms regularise with hierarchies and sign checks and validate with experiments ([Bijmolt et al. 2005](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmkr.42.2.141.62296); [Villas-Boas & Winer 1999](https://ideas.repec.org:443/a/inm/ormnsc/v45y1999i10p1324-1338.html)). The numbers come from mostly US and European studies. No open, citable Indian FMCG elasticity estimates were found; an Indian demo should treat them as priors, not local truths.

## What to build into a simulation-only demo

The demo has no trained model, so it should be honest that it demonstrates the *method*, not a forecast. The research team's design, built on Meridian and Robyn's inputs and the knapsack literature, is: a total offer budget; 3 to 5 customer groups, each with a response curve defined by two sliders (how much extra sales is available, how quickly it saturates) and a margin on extra sales; an offer type and depth selector; optional per-group minimum and maximum spend; a "minimum return per rupee" threshold; and a toggle between "use the whole budget" and "spend only while worthwhile" (mirroring Meridian's fixed and flexible budget modes; [Meridian scenarios](https://developers.google.com/meridian/docs/user-guide/budget-optimization-scenarios)). The math is: extra sales from group g with spend x is Smax × (1 − e^(−x/k)); net profit is margin × extra sales − x; the optimizer funds each group until the return on its next rupee equals a common value found by bisection. This is a tested design, not an industry standard: the formulas and numbers are the research team's own computation.

The worked example shows the story a manager can follow. With invented parameters for four groups and a Rs 100,000 budget, an equal split yields net profit of **Rs 20,651**; the optimal whole-budget plan (Rs 0 to loyal regulars, who mostly buy anyway, and about Rs 43k, 28k and 29k to the others) yields **Rs 28,349** (+37%); and the "spend only while each rupee earns at least a rupee" plan spends just **Rs 65,952** and earns **Rs 34,048**, because the last rupees of a forced full-budget spend lose money. Profit peaks near Rs 66k to 70k of budget, which is the "right-size the budget" insight (computed illustration; all figures invented). A second table in the notes shows why response alone misleads: per 1,000 lapsed customers, a buy-one-get-one offer attracts the most buyers (200) yet loses Rs 9,500 against no offer at a 30% margin, while 10% off gains Rs 300 and 20% off loses Rs 100. The BOGO break-even rule (margin of at least 50% for identical items) is simple algebra, cross-checked against a restaurant-industry blog rather than a primary source ([Eatery Club](https://www.eatery.club/en/blog/the-bogo-promotions-math)). This mirrors BCG's finding that 20 to 50 percent of promotions generate no noticeable lift or hurt, and 20 to 30 percent dilute margins (a 2015, US-oriented consultancy article; [BCG](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2015/retail-pricing-how-retailers-can-improve-promotion-effectiveness)).

Interface and wording choices follow the commercial tools. Meridian's Scenario Planner shows an optimised-versus-current summary table, change in spend by channel, response curves with current and optimal points marked, and a configuration table reviewed before running ([Meridian Scenario Planner](https://developers.google.com/meridian/docs/scenario-planning/meridian-scenario-planner)); Robyn shows initial-versus-optimised allocation and response with marked spend points ([Robyn analyst's guide](https://facebookexperimental.github.io/Robyn/docs/analysts-guide-to-MMM/)). The recommended panels are a current-versus-recommended plan card, spend by group with change bars, per-group curves with two dots, a "return on the next rupee" chart whose funded groups meet at one level (visual proof of optimality), and a budget-versus-profit curve with its peak. In plain wording, replace "baseline" with "sales that would have happened anyway", "uplift" with "extra sales caused by the offer", and "marginal ROI" with "return on the next rupee", and avoid calling the engine a prediction or an AI model. Because a demo can silently produce nonsense, build automated checks: spend sums to the budget (or less in threshold mode), each group stays within its bounds, funded groups have equal next-rupee return, curves are increasing and flattening, the recommended plan is never worse than the current one, and the water-filling, greedy and dynamic-programming solvers agree (in the research test: 28,349, 28,348 and 28,209, the last on a coarser grid). Robyn's authors themselves warn that allocator output must be validated before use ([Robyn analyst's guide](https://facebookexperimental.github.io/Robyn/docs/analysts-guide-to-MMM/)). For a first version, a Python backend is optional: the allocation runs instantly in plain JavaScript, and SciPy's SLSQP or PuLP can handle caps, floors or a discrete offer menu if needed ([SciPy](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.optimize.minimize.html); [PuLP](https://coin-or.github.io/pulp/)). Heavy uplift libraries (CausalML, scikit-uplift) belong only on an explanatory slide about how real systems would estimate group responses ([CausalML](https://github.com/uber/causalml)).

One item to settle with the manager: the integration notes' sample disclaimer names a specific retailer ("Reliance Fresh"). Unless the demo is intentionally branded for that retailer, use neutral wording such as "sample customer groups and rupee values you can edit; nothing here is learned from real data". For granularity, the defensible demo choice (inferred) is customer group by offer or category at the decision level, with a one-line note that the real world rolls category decisions down to SKUs by sales share.

## Conclusion

The research changes the framing from "predict who will buy" to "find where one more rupee changes behaviour", which is also what makes the demo's story credible: groups who would buy anyway receive nothing, the highest-response offer can be the biggest loser, and spending the full budget is sometimes worse than spending less. The sourced core (incremental targeting, knapsack or Lagrangian allocation, response curves with equal marginal returns, hierarchical estimation with group-level decisions, elasticity as a learned curve) is consistent across independent companies, which gives more confidence in the pattern than in any single reported lift.

The limits should be said out loud to the manager. Every business result cited is self-reported, no source documents CLV driving a promotion budget, there is no primary evidence on named retailers' SKU-versus-category practice or on Indian retailers (Meituan in China is the nearest documented analog), and the demo's formulas, worked numbers and checklist are the research team's design rather than industry standards. A one-slide "what this demo is and is not" will protect the presenter better than any single statistic.
