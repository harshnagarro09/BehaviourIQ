# Turn customer behaviour into profitable promotions

**Executive summary.** Customer behaviour predicts promotion outcomes in retail in two stages, and the second stage is where the money is: first, purchase, promotion-history, price, basket and timing signals feed models that predict who will respond and how much a promoted item will sell; second, industry practice converts those predictions into profitable action by estimating what an offer *changes* rather than who will buy. **The one-sentence answer is that behaviour data should be used to find the customers whose purchases are changed by an offer (uplift modelling and its four customer groups), proven with a control group (causal ML and incrementality measurement), spread across a fixed promotion budget (a knapsack optimisation), and kept current by controlled experimentation (contextual bandits).** The reason for the second stage is that a response model ranks people by probability to buy, so it selects "Sure Things" who would have bought anyway. In one published bank case the top 30% by response score produced an uplift of -0.004% and a profit of -$540,000 per million customers mailed, while a direct uplift model earned +$1.3 million per million ([Radcliffe](https://stochasticsolutions.com/pdf/CrossSell.pdf)). The prediction layer itself is mature and well evidenced: gradient-boosted trees won the M5 Walmart demand-forecasting competition ([Amazon Science](https://cdn.amazon.science/17/31/0d99831d4add92e711a56abc75f8/forecasting-with-trees.pdf)) and are the usual choice for coupon response, with model choice mattering less than feature engineering and leakage control. Evidence is strongest from technology and travel firms (Uber, Booking.com, Alibaba, Meituan, DoorDash) and thin from grocers; no public uplift or bandit case study from Tesco, Kroger, Walmart or an Indian grocer was found in this research, and the report does not claim one. Evidence on which behavioural features matter most is also thin and mostly practitioner testimony, and every formula and worked example here is an illustration, not a benchmark. Regulation (India's DPDP Act, US FTC scrutiny of "surveillance pricing", the Competition Commission of India's AI study) constrains how behaviour data may be used, and the report flags where sources disagree.

## The storyline: ten steps from business problem to measurement loop

The report is organised as one argument in which each step exposes the weakness of the step before it. A reader who accepts that prediction is not enough (step 5) has already asked for uplift (step 6), and so on. The table is the spine for a presentation: open each step with the question it answers and close with the one-line takeaway.

| Step | Section | Question answered | One-line takeaway |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Business problem | Are promotions profitable? | Promotions are expensive and often unprofitable, so the question is "did profit rise?", not "did sales rise?" |
| 2 | Behaviour data | What do we know about each shopper? | Transactions, offer logs, prices, baskets and timing describe each shopper; demographics add little. |
| 3 | Features | How do we turn data into signals? | Recency, frequency, promo share, category affinity and cycle position are the workhorse features, always computed from data before the offer date. |
| 4 | Prediction tasks | What exactly do we predict? | "Prediction" means at least six tasks (propensity, churn timing, next-best-offer, promo demand forecast, elasticity, customer lifetime value) with different owners and metrics. |
| 5 | Why prediction is not enough | Does a good model make money? | A response model finds likely buyers, many of whom would have bought anyway; a high-AUC model can destroy value. |
| 6 | Uplift: four customer groups | Who buys *because of* the offer? | Persuadables create new sales; Sure Things only receive subsidy; Lost Causes waste contact cost; Sleeping Dogs are harmed. |
| 7 | Causal measurement | How do we know the offer caused the sale? | Only a control group or a credible counterfactual turns "would have sold anyway" from a guess into a measurement. |
| 8 | Budget optimisation | Who gets which offer for the money available? | Fund the steps with the highest marginal return on investment (ROI) and stop where the return falls below the hurdle rate. |
| 9 | Contextual bandits | How does the system keep learning? | Try offers on small slices, shift towards winners per customer context, within safety limits and with a reward measured in incremental margin. |
| 10 | Measurement loop and governance | How do we stay honest and lawful? | Every wave keeps a holdout; consent, fairness, drift and contact limits wrap the whole system. |

A useful framing, which is the analyst's own synthesis and not a sourced maturity model, is a four-rung ladder: descriptive (segments and RFM scores), predictive (response and demand models), prescriptive (uplift plus a budget-constrained offer choice) and adaptive (closed-loop learning with permanent holdouts). Steps 2 to 5 sit on the first two rungs and steps 6 to 10 on the last two.

Step 1 deserves care because the headline statistic that "most promotions lose money" is weaker than it sounds. It traces to **Nielsen figures reported by Marketing Week in June 2015: up to 58% of UK grocery promotions and 60% across seven Western markets were not profitable for FMCG (fast-moving consumer goods) brands** ([Marketing Week](https://www.marketingweek.com/2015/06/18/over-half-of-uk-retail-promotions-are-loss-making-for-brands/)). That is a supplier-profit measure, not a retailer-profit measure, and the sample and the profitability definition were not disclosed. Higher figures (59-72%, "70%") circulate on blogs and vendor pages, and one blog concedes it could not trace its range to a primary source ([Koji](https://www.koji.so/blog/trade-promotion-effectiveness-research-2026)). A McKinsey figure of 72% of US promotions not turning a profit appears second-hand via TELUS ([TELUS](https://www.telus.com/agcg/en-us/blog-resources/trade-promotion-roi)) and carries the same caution. The safe line for a manager is: published industry statistics suggest a large share of promotions do not pay back for suppliers, the exact share is uncertain, and that uncertainty is itself the reason to measure.

## A business glossary: eight distinctions that carry most of the vocabulary

Much of the "proper business terminology" gap reduces to a few distinctions. **Baseline** is what would have sold without the promotion. **Gross lift** is promoted sales minus baseline. **Incremental** sales are lift after removing cannibalisation and pull-forward. **Subsidy** is the discount given on baseline sales. **ROI** is incremental profit over promotion cost. Response, conversion and redemption are different funnel stages. Segments describe people, while uplift groups describe responses. Predictions are not measurements. Definitions marked (GK) are standard industry or textbook usage from general knowledge, not from a single fetched source.

| Term | Plain meaning | Formula or tiny example |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline | Sales that would have happened with no promotion; the counterfactual ([Tredence](https://www.tredence.com/blog/decoding-the-metrics-a-deep-dive-into-calculating-promotion-effectiveness)) | Baseline 100 units a week; promo week 250 |
| Gross lift | Promoted sales minus baseline, before side effects | 250 - 100 = 150 units |
| Incremental sales | Lift that is truly new after cannibalisation and pull-forward (GK) | 150 - 40 (post-promo dip) - 30 (sister pack) = 80 units |
| Cannibalisation | Promoting item X reduces sales of item Y ([Tredence](https://www.tredence.com/blog/decoding-the-metrics-a-deep-dive-into-calculating-promotion-effectiveness)) | A deal on one own-label pack pulls buyers off a sister pack |
| Halo | Promoting X lifts a complementary item (GK) | A pasta deal lifts pasta-sauce sales |
| Pull-forward (post-promo dip) | Shoppers buy early, so later purchases fall (GK) | A shopper buys four weeks of tea in week 1 |
| Subsidy / margin leakage | Discount given to people who would have paid full price (GK) | 22,000 Sure Things x Rs 20 given away = Rs 4.4 lakh |
| Deal depth | Size of the discount (GK) | (regular - promo price) / regular price; 20% off = 0.20 |
| Promo ROI | Incremental profit over promotion cost (GK) | (incremental gross margin - promo cost) / promo cost |
| Price elasticity | % change in quantity per % change in price (GK) | -2 means a 10% price cut lifts volume about 20% |
| Response, conversion, redemption, breakage | Engaged with an offer; bought; used the offer; offers never redeemed (GK) | 1,000 sent, 300 opened, 120 bought, 100 redeemed; breakage is 900 of 1,000 |
| RFM | Recency, frequency, monetary value of a customer's purchases (GK) | Last trip 6 days ago, 9 trips in 90 days, Rs 18,000 spend: R=6, F=9, M=Rs 2,000 a trip |
| CLV | Customer lifetime value: expected future margin per customer (GK) | Used to set acquisition and retention spend |
| Reference price | The price a shopper expects to pay, shaped by past prices ([Kalyanaram and Winer](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v14y1995i3_supplementpg161-g169.html)) | Offer looks like a "gain" if below it |
| Propensity | Predicted probability that a customer takes an action, here P(buy given offer) (GK) | 0.15 means 15% chance |
| Calibration | Whether predicted probabilities match observed frequencies ([scikit-learn](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/calibration.html)) | Of customers scored 0.15, about 15% should respond |
| Data leakage | Using information in training that would not exist at prediction time (GK) | A 12-month spend total that includes weeks after the offer |
| Treatment, control, holdout | Customers who get the offer; customers deliberately withheld; a persistent control group (GK) | Withhold the offer from a random 10% of loyalty members |
| Uplift, ATE, CATE | Treated buy rate minus control buy rate ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uplift_modelling)); average effect over everyone (ATE); effect for customers sharing characteristics (CATE) ([MetricGate](https://metricgate.com/docs/uplift-modeling/)) | 30% - 24% = 6 percentage points |
| Persuadable, Sure Thing, Lost Cause, Sleeping Dog | The four uplift groups ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uplift_modelling)) | Table in Method 1 |
| Qini curve, AUUC | Chart and area score for judging whether an uplift model ranks customers correctly ([MetricGate](https://metricgate.com/docs/uplift-modeling/)) | Area above the random diagonal; higher is better |
| Next-best-offer (NBO) | The offer chosen for one customer from several candidates, given rules, scores and constraints (GK) | Choose between 10% off, bonus pack and free delivery |
| Contact policy, offer fatigue | A cap on offers per customer per period; declining response from over-exposure (GK) | Maximum two offers a week, with a "no offer" option |
| Knapsack / MCKP | Choose items of different cost and value to maximise value within a budget; "multiple-choice" means at most one offer depth per customer ([Zhou et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.15728)) | Maximise incremental profit with spend within budget |
| Marginal ROI, shadow price | Extra profit from the next rupee of budget (analyst derivation) | 0.26 to 0.38 at the cutoff in the Method 3 example |
| Contextual bandit | An algorithm that picks one offer per decision using customer context and learns from the result ([Braze](https://www.braze.com/resources/articles/what-is-offerfit)) | Thompson sampling over three offers |
| Exploration, exploitation, regret | Trying unproven offers; using the best known offer; profit lost against the best offer in hindsight (GK) | 10% of customers get a random offer |
| EDLP vs Hi-Lo | Everyday low price versus high price with frequent deep promotions (GK) | Context for promotion strategy |
| TPE / TPO | Trade promotion effectiveness and optimisation: the supplier-side view of promotion profit and planning (GK) | Optimise product-week-mechanic combinations |

## The behaviour layer: tables, features and the psychology behind them

### Five tables carry almost every promotion-response model

Competition-grade and academic models are built from a small set of tables: customer transactions (loyalty-card or panel), an item master, an offer-to-item mapping, a campaign calendar and, optionally, demographics. The AmExpert 2019 retail hackathon, a public benchmark, supplied exactly this: coupons offered to customers across 18 campaigns, campaign data for 28 campaigns, a coupon-item mapping, demographics "for some customers", transaction data, item data and a test set of the next 10 campaigns, scored on area under the ROC curve (AUC) ([Analytics Vidhya](https://www.analyticsvidhya.com/blog/2019/10/5-winning-solutions-approaches-amexpert-retail_hackathon-feature-engineering/)). In a causal study of the related Kaggle coupon data (1,582 customers, 50,624 customer-period observations, January 2012 to July 2013), about 30% received at least one coupon, only about 3% of coupon-recipient observations redeemed, and socio-economic fields had "many values unknown" ([arXiv 2204.10820](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.10820)). Two lessons follow. The label (redeemed or not) is rare and must be tied to what the customer was actually shown, so offer-level logs matter most. And demographics are often sparse, so behavioural history carries the model.

Identified shoppers (loyalty card, app login) support all customer-history features, while anonymous shoppers can only be analysed at store, day or basket level (analyst inference). Because any single SKU is bought rarely by a given customer, features are usually rolled up to brand and category levels; the AmExpert winners computed item, brand and category aggregates ([Analytics Vidhya](https://www.analyticsvidhya.com/blog/2019/10/5-winning-solutions-approaches-amexpert-retail_hackathon-feature-engineering/)). The classic scanner-panel studies (IRI ground coffee, 12 grocery categories) remain the academic source of brand-choice, price and promotion variables ([Grover and Srinivasan 1992](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/evaluating-multiple-effects-retail-promotions-brand-loyal-brand); [Bell and Lattin 2000](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/looking-loss-aversion-scanner-panel-data)). No verifiable source gave the share of grocery sales that is identified by a loyalty card in any market.

### Feature families, defined plainly

Features cluster into six groups. The definitions below are standard practitioner constructions, not quotations from a paper, and exact forms vary by team. Each is computed at a reference date t0, the offer date, using only data before t0.

| Family | Feature and plain definition | Tiny example | Why it predicts response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Value and timing | **Recency, frequency, monetary (RFM):** days since last purchase; trips in the last 90 days; spend per trip | R = 6, F = 9, M = Rs 2,000 | Active, high-value customers are reachable; they may also be Sure Things |
| Value and timing | **Inter-purchase time (IPT) and cycle position:** mean days between category purchases; days since last purchase divided by IPT | IPT 14, last bought 12 days ago: 12/14 = 0.86, "due soon" | Offers land near replenishment; very early offers risk stockpiling |
| What and how they buy | **Basket size and composition:** items per trip, average basket value, share of fresh or private label | Rs 1,200 basket, 35% fresh | Separates stock-up from top-up missions (inference) |
| What and how they buy | **Category affinity and share of wallet:** category spend over total spend; spend with us over the customer's total category spend | Dairy Rs 600 of Rs 4,000 = 15% | Offers on bought categories convert but may subsidise; offers on low-share categories test expansion |
| What and how they buy | **Brand loyalty and private-label share:** top-brand share of category purchases; private-label spend over total | 7 of 10 purchases = 0.7 | Loyal buyers and switchers react differently ([Grover and Srinivasan](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/evaluating-multiple-effects-retail-promotions-brand-loyal-brand)) |
| Promotion response | **Promotion proneness:** deal purchases over total purchases; **redemption rate:** redeemed over received offers | 12 of 40 = 30%; 3 of 20 = 15% | The most direct behavioural signal of response |
| Price sensitivity | **Elasticity proxy:** % change in units over % change in price per customer-category; **discount depth history; reference price:** recency-weighted average of past paid prices, RP(t) = a x RP(t-1) + (1 - a) x Price(t-1) | +30% units on -20% price = -1.5 | Targets "who responds to how much discount" |
| Stockpiling and switching | **Quantity jump:** deal-trip units over usual units; **post-deal dip:** lower purchase rate in following weeks; **deal-driven switch:** non-usual brand bought only on deal | 6 vs 2 units = 3.0x | Separates incremental volume from pull-forward |
| Timing and channel | Weekday or weekend share, day-of-month bucket, hour bin, channel share (app, store, quick commerce) | Share of trips on days 24 to 31 | Payday or time-of-month budgets (no verified source for the effect) |
| Lifecycle and exposure | **Tenure:** days since first purchase; lapsing if recency exceeds twice the IPT; **recent exposure:** offers in the last 7 and 30 days, days since last offer | New if under 90 days | Lifecycle state and fatigue shape response |

For sparse histories, practitioners shrink customer rates towards the population mean, for example (redeemed + k x global rate) / (offers + k), and fall back to segment or store priors for new customers (analyst inference). Several window lengths (7, 30, 90, 365 days) plus time-decayed versions are common. The AmExpert winners also used latent customer-item vectors from singular value decomposition (SVD) as a sparsity workaround ([Analytics Vidhya](https://www.analyticsvidhya.com/blog/2019/10/5-winning-solutions-approaches-amexpert-retail_hackathon-feature-engineering/)).

### Which features matter most: honestly, the evidence is thin

The only first-hand ranking found is practitioner testimony. In the AmExpert hackathon, the Rank 5 team reported item-level aggregations as the most important feature group, Rank 1 used permutation importance to cut 300+ features to 107, Rank 3 built propensity features (probability a customer buys under a given coupon at item, brand and category level) and said "Feature Creation is the key", and Rank 2 ranked coupons by overlap with a customer's previously bought items ([Analytics Vidhya](https://www.analyticsvidhya.com/blog/2019/10/5-winning-solutions-approaches-amexpert-retail_hackathon-feature-engineering/)). Two published coupon studies report no feature-importance table ([Pusztova and Babic](https://www.aei.tuke.sk/papers/2020/3/2_Pusztova.pdf); [arXiv 2204.10820](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.10820)). The statement "past response and recency dominate" is therefore plausible practitioner consensus, not a proven finding, and no peer-reviewed ranking of RFM against promotion-history features was found. Dunnhumby's vendor claims add colour but are single-case: CRM members were 19% more likely than average to be very price sensitive and 70% more likely to buy with a coupon, and demographics did not predict price sensitivity ([DataIQ](https://www.dataiq.co.uk/articles/dont-be-sensitive-about-loyal-customers)). The same source reports a 23.31% sales uplift for the least price-sensitive group on a frozen-sandwich coupon against 1.45% for the very price-sensitive group, attributed to existing penetration. That is a hint that heavy discount-takers can be poor targets, but it is a vendor case.

### Behavioural-science concepts behind the features

Five ideas explain why the features above work. **Reference price** says shoppers judge a price against an internal benchmark built from past prices, and that losses (price above reference) loom larger than gains ([Kalyanaram and Winer 1995](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v14y1995i3_supplementpg161-g169.html)); discount depth versus recency-weighted past price operationalises it. **Loss aversion in grocery is contested.** Bell and Lattin (2000) argued that scanner-panel loss-aversion estimates are inflated by unmodelled differences in price sensitivity between households; controlling for them reduced the estimates, often to non-significance, across orange juice and 11 other grocery categories, and they concluded loss aversion "may not be a universal phenomenon, at least for frequently purchased grocery products" ([Bell and Lattin](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/looking-loss-aversion-scanner-panel-data)). The practical consequence is to prefer customer-level price-sensitivity features over a single global asymmetry assumption (analyst inference). **Mental accounting** (Thaler 1985) splits deal value into acquisition utility and transaction utility, the perceived quality of the deal against a reference price ([AcaWiki summary](https://acawiki.org/Mental_Accounting_and_Consumer_Choice); a secondary summary that does not itself discuss coupons). **Framing**: Chen, Marmorstein, Tsiros and Rao (2012) found "base value neglect": in a 16-week field test of a 9 oz lotion at $13.50, a 50%-more-free pack sold 3.38 units a week against 1.88 for the economically better 35% off, about 73% more, but the advantage vanished for small or easy-to-convert offers (11% more vs 10% off; 100% more vs 50% off), expensive products and unfamiliar brands ([Chen et al.](https://carlsonschool.umn.edu/sites/carlsonschool.umn.edu/files/2024-06/Chen%2C%20Marmorstein%2C%20Tsiros%20and%20Rao%202012.pdf)). The **left-digit effect** appears when leftmost digits differ ($2.99 versus $3.00) ([Thomas and Morwitz 2005](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v32y2005i1p54-64.html)). Finally, **stockpiling and acceleration** after promotions were established by Neslin, Henderson and Quelch (1985); only bibliographic confirmation was retrieved ([London Business School](https://www.london.edu/faculty-and-research/academic-research/c/consumer-promotions-and-the-acceleration-of-product-purchases)). The mapping to features (offer type as framing flag, price ending as left-digit flag, quantity jump as stockpiling, promo share as deal proneness) is the analyst's, and Chen et al.'s boundary conditions mean percent-off versus extra-quantity framing in rupee pricing should be tested, not assumed.

Where promotion volume goes is itself a behavioural fact. Bell, Chiang and Padmanabhan (1999; 173 brands, 13 categories) found on average about 75% of promotional *elasticity* is secondary demand (switching) and 25% primary demand (acceleration and quantity), while Gupta (1988, coffee) found 84% brand switching, 14% purchase incidence and 2% quantity ([Bell et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v18y1999i4p504-526.html)). Those are shares of elasticity, not of unit sales; on units, the Van Heerde decomposition (Method 2) gives a much more even split.

### Feature practice: windows and leakage

Every AmExpert winner restricted historical features to transactions before each campaign's start date, and Rank 2 validated on the last three training campaigns, a time-based split ([Analytics Vidhya](https://www.analyticsvidhya.com/blog/2019/10/5-winning-solutions-approaches-amexpert-retail_hackathon-feature-engineering/)). Point-in-time correctness means each training row gets feature values as of its own event time; joining today's 12-month aggregate to a six-month-old event leaks the future and gives good offline but poor production results, which is why practitioners recommend feature stores that enforce the as-of join ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/vaibhav7387/feature-stores-and-point-in-time-correctness-the-bug-that-silently-ruins-ml-models-51k4); a practitioner blog). Interestingly, Rank 5 found ordinary k-fold beat group k-fold on the leaderboard, which may reflect shared customers between train and test rather than a better method (analyst inference).

## The prediction layer: six tasks, the models that fit them, and how to judge them

### "Prediction" is at least six different jobs

Calling the work "promotion prediction" hides that the target, grain, owner and error metric differ by task. The mapping to owners is the analyst's framing; the data points are sourced.

| Task | Plain question | Typical owner | Target and grain | Evidence in the notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Propensity | Which customers will redeem or buy if sent this offer? | CRM, loyalty | Yes/no, per customer-offer; rare (about 3% redemption) | Kaggle coupon data ([arXiv 2204.10820](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.10820)); DMC 2015 ([Pusztova and Babic](https://www.aei.tuke.sk/papers/2020/3/2_Pusztova.pdf)) |
| Churn and timing | Who is lapsing, and when will they next shop? | CRM | Probability of being active; expected future transactions | BG/NBD uses only frequency, recency and observation length ([Fader, Hardie, Lee](https://www.brucehardie.com/papers/bgnbd_2004-04-20.pdf)) |
| Next-best-offer | Which product or offer should this customer see? | CRM, digital, merchandising | Ranked offers per customer | Decision-layer practice ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/next-best-experience-how-ai-can-power-every-customer-interaction)) |
| Promo demand forecast | How many units will this SKU sell in this store this week on this deal? | Category, promo planning, supply chain | Units, per SKU-store-week; baseline plus lift | M5: 42,840 series, 30,490 at item-store level ([UNIC](https://pure.unic.ac.cy/en/publications/m5-accuracy-competition-results-findings-and-conclusions/)); Rossmann ([Kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/c/rossmann-store-sales/discussion/18024)) |
| Elasticity | By what % does volume move per % price change? | Pricing, category | A slope per product or customer | Not sourced in this track; see Method 2 for causal estimation |
| Customer lifetime value | How much future margin will this customer generate? | CRM, finance | Currency per customer | Not sourced in the notes beyond the BG/NBD family |

Cannibalisation, a sub-task of promo demand forecasting, can be defined as the ratio of a substitute product's volume drop to the promoted product's volume uplift ([Herrala 2018](https://sal.aalto.fi/publications/pdf-files/ther18_public.pdf)). The base-rate point matters: a 3%-positive classification label and a continuous weekly-volume target need different metrics, which is why accuracy suits neither.

### Models: what is standard, and what the evidence actually supports

For customer response on tabular data, regularised logistic regression is the interpretable, well-calibrated baseline and gradient-boosted decision trees (GBDT) are the usual accuracy upgrade. The scikit-learn documentation notes logistic regression is usually well calibrated while random forests give under-confident probabilities at the extremes and Naive Bayes pushes probabilities towards 0 and 1 ([scikit-learn](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/calibration.html)). For timing and repeat purchase with sparse histories, BG/NBD and Pareto/NBD are the classic structural models: BG/NBD ties dropout to purchase events and can be fitted in a spreadsheet solver; on CDNOW data (2,357 customers) it matched Pareto/NBD closely, and for 1,411 customers with no repeat purchases predicted 0.23 future transactions against 0.24 actual ([Fader, Hardie, Lee](https://www.brucehardie.com/papers/bgnbd_2004-04-20.pdf)). A grocery comparison found similar individual-level forecasts but diverging aggregate forecasts, possibly from seasonality handling ([Batislam et al.](https://research.sabanciuniv.edu/793)).

For demand forecasting the evidence is strongest. In the M5 competition on Walmart sales, the winners of both the accuracy and uncertainty tracks used gradient-boosted trees; neural nets (DeepAR, N-BEATS) placed second and third in the accuracy track, but GBDT entries vastly outnumbered them ([Amazon Science](https://cdn.amazon.science/17/31/0d99831d4add92e711a56abc75f8/forecasting-with-trees.pdf)). The expert commentary credits a "cross-learning" effect, one model trained across many series, and calls it machine learning's first clear win in the M-competitions ([Durham/IJF](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1207845)). In the Rossmann case, entity embeddings (learned numeric vectors for categories such as store or weekday) helped a third-place finish; in the authors' controlled test on time-ordered data the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) was 0.290 for k-nearest neighbours, 0.158 for random forest, 0.152 for XGBoost and 0.101 for a neural net, and feeding the learned embeddings into the other models cut their errors (XGBoost 0.115, random forest 0.108) ([Guo and Berkhahn](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.06737)). The source itself cautions the experiments used fewer features than the winning solution.

For coupon redemption on a small shop dataset (DMC 2015, 6,722 orders), accuracy on coupon 3 was 91.5% for a support vector machine (SVM), 91.3% for C5.0, 90.7% for logistic regression and 90.3% for random forest, with SVM AUC 0.94, and data preparation improved every model ([Pusztova and Babic](https://www.aei.tuke.sk/papers/2020/3/2_Pusztova.pdf)). Differences of one to two points on one small dataset do not establish a universal ranking; the consistent lesson is that data preparation and feature engineering move results more than model choice. Two cautions apply to M5: it covers everyday items with price and event covariates, so it does not by itself prove GBDT is best for deep-discount events with sparse history (analyst inference), and hybrids of econometric elasticity structure with machine-learning residuals are commonly described by vendors but no primary source was retrieved. Multinomial logit and hierarchical Bayesian choice models, sequence models on purchase histories and survival models for timing were not sourced.

### Evaluation: why accuracy misleads, and what to use instead

With 3% responders, a model that predicts "no one redeems" is about 97% accurate and useless (the analyst's arithmetic from the sourced 3% rate). In the DMC study, logistic regression "often failed to predict the redeemed class" on unprepared data and Naive Bayes accuracy fell to 23.6% and 27.7% on two coupons ([Pusztova and Babic](https://www.aei.tuke.sk/papers/2020/3/2_Pusztova.pdf)). The metric families below are standard; their business readings are the analyst's framing unless cited.

| Task | Metric | Plain reading | Tiny example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Response ranking | AUC (area under the ROC curve) | Chance a random responder is ranked above a random non-responder | AUC 0.94 = 94% |
| Response ranking | Lift and gain by decile; precision at k | How many times better than random the top 10% performs | 100,000 customers, 3% respond = 3,000 responders; top 10,000 contain 1,200, so lift = 12% / 3% = 4.0x and the top decile captures 40% of responders |
| Probability quality | Brier score, log loss, reliability diagram | Brier is mean squared error of probabilities; log loss penalises confident wrong answers; both mix calibration, discrimination and noise, so also plot reliability ([scikit-learn](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/calibration.html)) | Score 0.15 should mean about 15% respond |
| Demand forecast | WAPE and bias | WAPE is total absolute error over total actual units; bias is signed total error | Actual 120, 40, 0, 10; forecast 100, 50, 5, 10: errors 20+10+5+0 = 35 over 170 units, WAPE = 20.6%; bias = (165 - 170)/170 = -2.9% |
| Demand forecast | MASE, RMSSE | Error scaled by a naive benchmark's in-sample error; below 1 beats naive ([Hyndman, FPP3](https://otexts.com/fpp3/accuracy.html)) | RMSSE 0.8 beats seasonal naive |
| Value | Profit curve, incremental profit by decile | What the targeting rule earns after discount cost | Section on uplift |

MAPE is undefined or extreme when actual sales are zero and penalises over- and under-forecasts asymmetrically ([FPP3](https://otexts.com/fpp3/accuracy.html)), which is why the zero in the WAPE example would break it. The M5 uncertainty track used weighted quantile loss; at item-store level the winner scored 0.336 against 0.347 for DeepAR ([Amazon Science](https://cdn.amazon.science/17/31/0d99831d4add92e711a56abc75f8/forecasting-with-trees.pdf)). Calibration can be repaired with Platt (sigmoid) scaling, suited to small samples and ranking-preserving, or isotonic regression, which is more flexible but overfits below roughly 1,000 samples, always fitted on data the classifier did not train on ([scikit-learn](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/calibration.html)).

Validation must be time-ordered. In the Rossmann paper XGBoost's MAPE was 0.122 on a shuffled test set but 0.152 on a time-ordered one, so random splits flatter models ([Guo and Berkhahn](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.06737)). The biggest warning for a manager comes from the bank cross-sell case: ranking quality and business value can diverge completely, as the executive summary shows ([Radcliffe](https://stochasticsolutions.com/pdf/CrossSell.pdf); a vendor-author source and a single case).

### Explainability, labels and leakage

Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) assign each input an importance for a specific prediction, unifying six earlier methods ([Lundberg and Lee](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.07874)). For managers, the analyst suggests a global SHAP summary for "what drives response", top-three reason codes per customer ("recent category purchase", "price-sensitive history") and partial-dependence curves for discount depth, with the caution that SHAP explains the model, not causation. That caution matches the causal-forest paper's argument that predictive machine learning is unsuited to causal questions because of regularisation and selection bias ([arXiv 2204.10820](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.10820)).

Label design is where definitions bite. The sourced coupon papers show the risk: one reports 33 campaign periods in one section and T = 32 in another, and the DMC train and test counts (6,053 and 699) do not sum to 6,722 orders ([arXiv 2204.10820](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.10820); [Pusztova and Babic](https://www.aei.tuke.sk/papers/2020/3/2_Pusztova.pdf)). The Starbucks sample data distinguishes offers received, viewed and completed, and informational offers have no completion event, so "responded" must be defined ([Towards Data Science](https://towardsdatascience.com/starbucks-offer-dataset-udacity-capstone-7b562843ff47)). A checklist, which is the analyst's: fix the response window (7 or 14 days after sending); decide the attribution (any purchase, purchase of the promoted SKU, or redemption); decide the exposure (delivered or viewed); cut features off at the offer date so training matches scoring; and guard against features computed after the offer, future-dated aggregates and random k-fold on repeated customers. Out-of-stock and survivorship bias had to be handled before hierarchical methods helped in the Amazon write-up ([Amazon Science](https://cdn.amazon.science/17/31/0d99831d4add92e711a56abc75f8/forecasting-with-trees.pdf)). Refresh cadence (propensity per campaign cycle, forecasts weekly) is practice-based inference without a source.

## Segmentation and the customer lifecycle: useful descriptions, not decisions

### How segments are really built, and which labels are traceable

Retailers use several overlapping lenses: value tiers, lifecycle state, shopping mission, promotion behaviour and lifestage or lifestyle. Tesco and dunnhumby described profiling customers by lifestage, profitability, primary channel, lifestyles, "promotional promiscuity", shopping habits, shopping trips and brand advocacy, with segmentation covering lifestage, value and loyalty, and lifestyles ([Humby 2009](https://www.tescoplc.com/media/475966/winning_with_dunnhumby___clive_humby.pdf)). The best-documented mission study, at a Czech drugstore chain, clustered about 5.6 million baskets from 1.5 million loyalty customers by spend share across 55 categories into 12 basket clusters and then 18 customer segments; the segments differed substantially from RFM segments (highest purity score 0.52), and in an email test mission-based messages raised open rates by 3% to 11% and spend on promoted products by 2% to 6%, with authors flagging one retailer, three months and one campaign ([Sokol and Holy](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1909.02996)). The cluster counts were chosen using statistical indices plus interpretability judgement, which shows that "how many segments" is a judgement call.

Not every familiar label has a source. **Deal-prone** households trace to Blattberg and Sen (1978), who used panel data on five frequently purchased products and found deal-prone households identifiable, with the strongest predictors being household resources such as home and car ownership, which lower the storage and transaction costs of buying on deal ([Kellogg](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/1978/identifying-the-deal-prone-segment/?p=1)). **Brand-loyal and switching** segments trace to Grover and Srinivasan (1992), who found promotions shift share differently across them and that stockpiling after-effects last longer among brand-loyal segments ([Grover and Srinivasan](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/evaluating-multiple-effects-retail-promotions-brand-loyal-brand)). **Switcher, stockpiler** and related labels map to the decomposition of response into brand switching, acceleration and quantity ([Bell et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v18y1999i4p504-526.html)). But the popular four-way taxonomy "stockpilers, switchers, brand-loyal, non-responders" could not be traced to any single primary source and is best called practitioner shorthand. Indian quick-commerce operators also use recency-based segments; Blinkit's CleverTap case study (August 2021) describes segments of users who had not ordered in 15 to 30 days, plus brand-affinity and local-language segments, with reported retention up 6%, but it is a vendor case study, not independent evidence ([CleverTap](https://clevertap.com/case-study/blinkit-boosted-retention-by-6-by-delivering-a-great-cx/)).

### Why a descriptive segment is not the decision

A segment becomes decision-oriented only if its members should receive a different offer, channel, depth or contact frequency; if two segments get the same treatment, merge them (analyst inference). The Czech study illustrates the risk that statistically distinct segments produce small gains. Anderson and Simester supply a sharper warning: with RFM controls added, the promotion effect on established customers' future units shrank to an insignificant -0.014, meaning part of the apparent effect came from who received the promotion rather than the promotion ([Anderson and Simester 2004](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/anderson_e/htm/personalpage_files/Papers/Long_Run_Effects_of_Promotion_Depth_on_New_versus_Established_Customers.pdf)). Segments describe **who a customer is**; uplift groups describe **how that customer responds to a specific offer**. The same customer can be a Persuadable for one offer and a Sure Thing for another, so the segment label can inform the model as a feature and explain it as a persona, but the decision should use estimated uplift. "Deal-prone versus brand-loyal" is best treated as a score (share of spend on promotion, price paid versus list) rather than a fixed type, since Blattberg and Sen predicted it from household resources, not from permanent character.

### Lifecycle promotions with the best field evidence

| Lifecycle stage | Offer logic suggested by the evidence | Sourced finding | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acquisition and onboarding | Welcome discounts for first-time customers | Deeper promotions raised *future* purchases of first-time customers (future units index 114 and 134 in two studies) | One mail-order durable-goods retailer |
| Established, loyal | Be cautious with deep discounts | Deeper promotions *lowered* future units for established customers (index 90; future revenue $584.68 vs $733.50) | Transfer to grocery untested |
| Lapsed, win-back | Low reacquisition price, then higher prices after return | Optimal pricing "involves a low reacquisition price and higher prices when customers have been reacquired" | Model-based finding |
| Growth (cross-sell, category extension) | No sourced evidence found | None | Gap |

The first two rows come from three field experiments by Anderson and Simester (2004) at a mail-order catalog, in which customers were randomised to promotion or control catalogs and tracked for 22 to 28 months: for established customers the future units index was 90 (6.89 vs 7.67), and for first-time customers 114 (p<0.10) and 134 (1.33 vs 0.99, p<0.01). Forward buying explained only part of the pattern, and the authors argue short-run metrics overstate the demand change for established customers and understate it for new ones ([Anderson and Simester](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/anderson_e/htm/personalpage_files/Papers/Long_Run_Effects_of_Promotion_Depth_on_New_versus_Established_Customers.pdf)). The win-back finding is from Thomas, Blattberg and Fox (2004), who modelled reacquisition and the length of the second customer tenure together ([Kellogg](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2004/recapturing-lost-customers/)). Win-back offers should therefore be time-limited and followed by normal pricing (analyst reading). Blinkit's onboarding and 15-to-30-day lapse journeys are vendor-reported only.

### Promotion mechanics

Mechanics interact with behaviour. The Chen et al. results (above) show a percentage bonus pack can beat an equivalent discount through base value neglect, with clear boundary conditions. Theory says stockpilers respond to depth and multi-buy but cause pull-forward, switchers respond to depth on competing brands, and loyal buyers redeem on purchases they would have made anyway (standard reasoning, not evidenced by sources retrieved). Tesco's Clubcard mixed vouchers (redemption over 90%) with product offers (over 20%) and till-delivered offers (typically 17.5%), used about 7 million variations of product coupons across about 15 million customers a quarter, and relied on supplier-funded coupon sourcing ([Humby 2009](https://www.tescoplc.com/media/475966/winning_with_dunnhumby___clive_humby.pdf)); this means a decision engine must also handle supplier commitments. No sourced evidence was found on BOGO versus multi-buy, bundles, thresholds, loyalty multipliers, or personalised versus mass offer effectiveness.

## The four industry methods, at the same depth

The four sections below answer the same prompts in the same order: what it is, the business question, how it works, a worked example, data needed, how it is evaluated, industry examples and limitations. The order follows the storyline: uplift, causal measurement, budget optimisation, contextual bandits. All worked examples use invented inputs with checked arithmetic and are teaching numbers only.

### Method 1: Uplift modelling and the four customer groups

**What it is.** Uplift modelling (also called incremental, true-lift or net modelling) predicts the incremental effect of an offer on an individual customer's behaviour, using both treated and control customers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uplift_modelling)). It estimates the conditional average treatment effect (CATE): P(buy if offered) minus P(buy if not offered).

**Business question.** "Who buys *because of* our offer?" A conventional response model uses only treated customers and never sees the control group, so it cannot separate Persuadables from Sure Things ([Radcliffe and Surry](https://stochasticsolutions.com/pdf/sig-based-up-trees.pdf)). In their bank example, targeting the top 30% by a response model produced "almost exactly zero incremental sales", while the uplift model's top 30% captured 90% of the incremental sales obtainable by targeting everyone. Uber's CausalML paper reports an internal analysis where targeting only 30% of users matched the conversion increase from promoting to all ([CausalML paper](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2002.11631)).

**How it works.** Each customer has two outcomes: buys without the offer and buys with it. Only one is ever observed for a real person, so group membership is latent and models estimate it. The four groups come from Radcliffe and are used by MetricGate, scikit-uplift, DoorDash ("always buyers" versus "persuadables") and a 2025 California Management Review (CMR) paper ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uplift_modelling); [MetricGate](https://metricgate.com/docs/uplift-modeling/); [DoorDash](https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/doordash-smarter-promotions-with-causal-machine-learning); [CMR](https://cmr-mig.berkeley.edu/assets/documents/pdf/2025-11-to-treat-or-not-to-treat-five-lessons-learned-from-using-uplift-modeling-to-optimize-marketing-campaigns.pdf)).

| Group | Buys without offer? | Buys with offer? | Effect | Business meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Persuadable | No | Yes | +1 | Buys only because of the offer (switcher, trial, lapsed or stock-up shopper) | Target |
| Sure Thing | Yes | Yes | 0 | Would buy anyway; the offer gives away margin | Do not discount |
| Lost Cause | No | No | 0 | Will not buy at any offer; only contact cost wasted | Do not contact |
| Sleeping Dog | Yes | No | -1 | Buys unprompted but is turned off by the offer | Avoid |

Modelling choices run from simple to sophisticated. The two-model approach (one model each for treated and control, then subtract) is simple but its errors compound, and the single-model approach (treatment as a feature) can ignore the treatment flag and report zero uplift for everyone ([Kunzel et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.03461); [MetricGate](https://metricgate.com/docs/uplift-modeling/)). Class transformation relabels data so an ordinary classifier learns uplift, but needs a 50/50 randomised split ([scikit-uplift](https://www.uplift-modeling.com/en/latest/user_guide/models/revert_label.html)). The X-learner suits unbalanced groups, typical when a grocer holds out a small control; uplift trees and causal forests model heterogeneity directly and causal forests give confidence intervals under unconfoundedness ([Wager and Athey](https://arxiv.org/abs/1510.04342)). Kunzel and colleagues conclude "none of the meta-learners is uniformly the best". The analyst's rule of thumb is to start with two-model and single-model baselines, move to the X-learner for unbalanced tests and use causal forests when intervals matter.

**Worked example (invented inputs; arithmetic checked).** Take 100,000 shoppers and a 20% offer. Treated customers buy at 30%, control at 24%, so uplift is 6 percentage points, or **6,000 incremental buyers**. The offer "worked" for 30,000 buyers, but only one in five was caused by it, so a report of "30% conversion" overstates impact five-fold. Now add money. Price is 100, unit margin 30 at full price and 10 after a 20% discount. Assume group sizes (invented, not a benchmark) of Persuadables 8%, Sure Things 22%, Lost Causes 68% and Sleeping Dogs 2%, which reproduces the 30% treated rate (8% + 22%) and the 24% control rate (22% + 2%). Persuadables add 8,000 x 10 = +80,000. Sure Things change from 30 to 10 margin: 22,000 x (10 - 30) = -440,000. Sleeping Dogs lose their full-price margin: 2,000 x 30 = -60,000. Lost Causes contribute zero. The blanket offer nets **-420,000** although uplift is positive, whereas an offer aimed only at Persuadables nets **+80,000**. The break-even rule follows: treat a customer only if P(buy if offered) x promo margin exceeds P(buy if not offered) x full margin; with margins of 10 and 30, the offered buy probability must be more than three times the unoffered one.

**Data needed.** A randomised treated-and-control experiment is the clean requirement. Radcliffe and Surry's rule is that overall uplift multiplied by group size should be at least 500, so a 0.5-point uplift needs about 100,000 customers per arm (500 / 0.005) and a 0.1-point uplift about 500,000 ([Radcliffe and Surry](https://stochasticsolutions.com/pdf/sig-based-up-trees.pdf)). A pilot of 20,000 will probably support only an average effect, not a reliable segment-level model (analyst inference from that rule). Several discount depths need a multi-arm test; Booking.com ran a three-discount A/B/N test and Didi's generalised causal forest handles continuous discount levels ([Booking.com](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.13298v1); [Didi](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.10975)).

**How it is evaluated.** AUC and accuracy are not valid for uplift, because AUC rewards ranking buyers above non-buyers, which a response model already does ([MetricGate](https://metricgate.com/docs/uplift-modeling/)). Instead, rank a held-out randomised sample by predicted uplift and check whether the treated-minus-control gap is largest at the top. The Qini curve plots cumulative incremental conversions against the share targeted; the Qini coefficient is the area between the model curve and the random diagonal; a curve tracking the diagonal means the model learned nothing. The manager-friendly version is a decile table. Illustratively (invented), on a holdout of 100,000 with 90% treated, the top decile might show treated 12.0% against control 6.0% (+6.0 points), the fifth decile 5.1% against 5.0% (+0.1) and the tenth 8.0% against 9.0% (-1.0, the Sleeping Dog zone); a healthy model declines steadily and turns negative at the bottom. Radcliffe and Surry also suggest subtracting 0.5 to 1.0 standard deviations of Qini across resamples as a pessimistic score. The plain rule is to trust the ranking, not the decimals.

**Industry examples.** Uber (CausalML), Booking.com (promotions such as room upgrades and free meals under an ROI constraint, trained on over 100 million interactions), Alibaba, Didi and Criteo have public detail ([CausalML paper](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2002.11631); [Booking.com RecSys 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2008.06293); [CanniUplift](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.05242)). Alibaba's CanniUplift reported an online A/B test with platform gross merchandise value (GMV) +4.08%, ROI +6.69% and marketing cost -2.45%. The public Criteo dataset (about 14 million rows, 85% treated, 0.29% conversion) is the standard benchmark ([Diemert et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2111.10106)). For grocery, dunnhumby says its technology identifies shoppers likely to respond to specific deals, but the article gave no test-versus-control method or results ([Supermarket News](https://www.supermarketnews.com/grocery-marketing/southeastern-grocers-eyes-more-effective-promotions)). Do not say Tesco, Kroger or Reliance use uplift modelling; no source supports it.

**Limitations.** Uplift is a second-order effect, the difference of two noisy rates and often small beside the main effect (for example 1% against 1.1%), so models are unstable ([Radcliffe and Surry](https://stochasticsolutions.com/pdf/sig-based-up-trees.pdf)); greedy trees can find spurious heterogeneity ([Unified survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.12769)). Sleeping Dogs are common in contract and renewal settings but "rare or absent" in many non-contractual ones ([CMR](https://cmr-mig.berkeley.edu/assets/documents/pdf/2025-11-to-treat-or-not-to-treat-five-lessons-learned-from-using-uplift-modeling-to-optimize-marketing-campaigns.pdf)), suggesting few in grocery; the CMR paper recommends testing for them with a small campaign. Interference matters: Alibaba found that summing per-seller uplift predictions overstated the observed effect by about 35%, because coupons for one shop shift spend from another. No source reports typical group sizes, so the 8/22/68/2 split must never be presented as a benchmark.

### Method 2: Causal ML and incrementality measurement

**What it is.** Incrementality is sales with the promotion minus the sales that would have happened anyway. Causal machine learning (causal ML) is the set of methods that estimate that gap credibly, from randomised experiments or from observational data with statistical correction. It supplies the estimates used inside uplift models.

**Business question.** "How do we know the promotion caused the sales?" This is the manager's most likely challenge, and the answer is a design, not a model.

**How it works.** Each customer has spend-if-promoted and spend-if-not, but only one is observed; the other, the counterfactual, must be estimated (GK). The ideal tool is the randomised controlled trial: withhold the offer from a random slice of loyalty members, or a random set of stores, and compare. An A/A test (same split, no change) should show no difference and validates the set-up (GK). Google runs geo experiments in which non-overlapping geographic areas are randomly assigned to treatment or control ([Vaver and Koehler](https://research.google/pubs/measuring-ad-effectiveness-using-geo-experiments/)). Kroger and 84.51 used controlled store tests with causal impact analysis; the promotion drew previously lapsed customers rather than significant numbers of new ones, and they projected $36 million incremental revenue if scaled ([84.51](https://www.8451.com/knowledge-hub/technology/measuring-the-impact-of-a-new-promotion-84-51-helped-kroger-derive/)). When randomising is impossible, quasi-experimental methods build a counterfactual: synthetic control weights comparison units ([Abadie et al.](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/59447)); CausalImpact forecasts the no-promotion outcome from control series using Bayesian structural time series ([Brodersen et al.](https://research.google/pubs/inferring-causal-impact-using-bayesian-structural-time-series-models/)); difference-in-differences compares before-and-after changes in promoted and unpromoted stores and needs parallel trends (GK). On top sit modern estimators: double machine learning (DML) with cross-fitting removes regularisation bias, gives valid confidence intervals and can estimate elasticities ([Chernozhukov et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.00060)); causal forests and the meta-learners give customer-level effects ([Kunzel et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03461); [Nie and Wager](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.04912)). Libraries include EconML, CausalML, grf and DoWhy, whose four steps are model, identify, estimate and refute ([EconML](https://github.com/py-why/EconML); [CausalML](https://github.com/uber/causalml); [grf](https://grf-labs.github.io/grf/); [DoWhy](https://github.com/py-why/dowhy)).

**Worked examples (invented; arithmetic checked).** First, the selection-bias trap. Loyalty coupons go to the 1,000 best customers, who spend Rs 500 a week anyway. The promoted group spends Rs 560 and everyone else Rs 200, so the naive lift is Rs 360. A randomised holdout of comparable best customers spends Rs 540, so true incremental spend is **Rs 20**, one-eighteenth of the naive figure (360 / 20 = 18). A model trained on past response would learn that heavy buyers love coupons and keep sending them to people who need them least. The causal-forest coupon study shows the same pattern in real data: a significant average effect of any coupon on daily spend (63.26, SE 4.553) hid large differences, with non-food coupons significantly negative (-26.77) ([arXiv 2204.10820](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.10820)). Second, the baseline-and-lift decomposition: baseline 100 units a week, promotion week 250, gross lift 150; subtract a 40-unit dip and 30 units cannibalised and incremental units are **80**. Third, the Van Heerde decomposition: across four store-level datasets the sales bump splits into cross-brand, cross-period and category-expansion effects of roughly one third each ([Van Heerde et al. 2004](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v23y2004i3p317-334.html)). A 300-unit bump would be about 100 units from other brands, 100 pulled from future weeks and 100 truly new demand; "one third each" is an average, and individual promotions vary. The often-quoted "75% brand switching" is a share of price elasticity, not of units; on units, switching is at most about a third on average ([Van Heerde, Gupta and Wittink](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ysm/somwrk/ysm222.html)).

**Data needed.** For experiments: a randomisation unit (household or loyalty card for personalised offers; store or geography for shelf promotions), a persistent never-offered control, a pre-set duration and enough sample for the expected effect. Meta's conversion-lift tests, as described by the vendor Haus, need at least 7 days and at least 10% of the audience in each cell ([Haus](https://www.haus.io/article/meta-incrementality-testing)). For observational methods: measured confounders, meaning everything that drove both who got the offer and the outcome. Causal forests are valid only under unconfoundedness, which cannot be tested from data ([Wager and Athey](https://arxiv.org/abs/1510.04342)).

**How it is evaluated.** Experiments are validated with A/A tests and by comparing treated and control groups on pre-period behaviour. Observational estimates are stress-tested with refutation checks such as adding a random common cause ([DoWhy](https://github.com/py-why/dowhy)) and placebo tests, where a promotion is pretended in an earlier period or on an unaffected product and the estimated effect should be near zero (GK). Report intervals, not point estimates.

**Industry examples.** Kroger and 84.51 store tests; Google geo experiments; Uber's CausalML and a multi-treatment uplift paper that chooses among options with different costs ([Zhao and Harinen](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.05372)); and DoorDash, which uses Double ML in both its case studies, with a targeted non-restaurant campaign reaching about the same incremental orders as a uniform baseline at roughly half the cost per incremental order ([DoorDash](https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/doordash-smarter-promotions-with-causal-machine-learning)). No first-party material from Walmart, Tesco/dunnhumby or Target on holdout practice was verified.

**Limitations.** Randomisation is the gold standard but costs money because the control group forgoes sales. Observational corrections fix only measured confounders. Spillovers between households break the stable-unit assumption ([MetricGate](https://metricgate.com/docs/uplift-modeling/)). Forecast-based methods depend on a good baseline that factors out seasonality, holidays and previous promotions or ROI misleads ([TELUS](https://www.telus.com/agcg/en-us/blog-resources/trade-promotion-roi)). For a retailer promoting its own label, switching within its own shelf nets to zero on volume but may raise margin mix (analyst inference).

### Method 3: Budget optimisation and the knapsack problem

**What it is.** The knapsack problem asks how to fill a bag of fixed capacity with items of different weight and value to maximise total value. In promotions, an item is a customer-offer pair, its value is expected incremental profit, its weight is expected discount spend and the bag is the budget. If each customer gets at most one of several offer depths, it is the multiple-choice knapsack problem (MCKP). Meituan states the binary assignment is a 0/1 knapsack and the multi-treatment version an MCKP, both computationally hard ([Zhou et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.15728)).

**Business question.** "Given a fixed promotion budget, who gets which offer, and how much budget is worth spending?"

**How it works.** Uplift models score each customer-offer pair. The optimiser then ranks the *steps* by marginal ROI, which is extra profit divided by extra spend, and funds the best steps until money runs out. Booking.com formulated it as a binary knapsack with a constraint that net financial loss be non-positive, solved greedily: sort customers by incremental outcome over incremental cost, treat until the constraint breaks, and the last customer treated defines a threshold ([Goldenberg et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2008.06293)). At scale the standard device is the Lagrangian: set a single "price of budget", let every customer independently pick the offer with the highest profit minus price times cost, and adjust the price by bisection until spend hits the budget; Alibaba used this for billions of decision variables ([Zhao et al.](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1902.01128)). Uber found a generic linear programming solver struggled at about 100,000 users and moved to Google OR-Tools CP-SAT, which solved the same data in minutes and scaled to millions ([Uber](https://www.uber.com/blog/solving-multiple-knapsack/)). Real systems add rules as constraints: total budget, per-offer limits and category restrictions ([DoorDash](https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/doordash-smarter-promotions-with-causal-machine-learning)); cost cap, profit floor and ROI floor ([Zhao et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.01128v2)).

**Worked example (the analyst's inputs; brute force over all 1,024 combinations and hand arithmetic both checked).** Every customer has a Rs 1,000 basket and 30% margin (Rs 300 before discount). Offers are 5%, 10%, 20% or nothing. Purchase probabilities for no offer / 5% / 10% / 20%: C1 0.20/0.26/0.32/0.36; C2 0.10/0.11/0.12/0.13; C3 0.30/0.38/0.46/0.52; C4 0.05/0.09/0.15/0.26; C5 0.40/0.41/0.42/0.43. Incremental profit is P(buy if offered) x (margin - discount) minus P(buy if not offered) x margin; expected spend is P(buy if offered) x discount x basket. For C4 at 10%: 0.15 x (300 - 100) - 0.05 x 300 = 30 - 15 = +15, with spend 0.15 x 100 = 15.

| Customer | 5% off (profit, spend) | 10% off | 20% off | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | +5.0, 13.0 | +4.0, 32.0 | -24.0, 72.0 | Mildly responsive; shallow offer only |
| C2 | -2.5, 5.5 | -6.0, 12.0 | -17.0, 26.0 | Barely moves; never worth an offer |
| C3 | +5.0, 19.0 | +2.0, 46.0 | -38.0, 104.0 | Responsive but expensive |
| C4 | +7.5, 4.5 | +15.0, 15.0 | +11.0, 52.0 | Persuadable; deeper is not better past 10% |
| C5 | -17.5, 20.5 | -36.0, 42.0 | -77.0, 86.0 | Would buy anyway; every offer loses money |

Ranking the upgrade steps by marginal ROI: C4 none to 5% gives 7.5 / 4.5 = 1.67; C4 5% to 10% gives 7.5 / 10.5 = 0.71; C1 none to 5% gives 5 / 13 = 0.38; C3 none to 5% gives 5 / 19 = 0.26; every other step is negative. With a budget of Rs 30 the greedy plan funds the first three steps: C1 at 5% and C4 at 10%, spending Rs 28 for **+Rs 20 profit**, which matches the brute-force optimum. With Rs 47 or more it adds C3 at 5% for **+Rs 25 profit** on Rs 47 of spend; more money buys nothing, because every remaining step loses money. C2 and C5 never receive an offer at any budget. The "shadow price" at the Rs 30 budget lies between 0.26 and 0.38, so one more rupee of budget returns about 30 paise of profit (strictly the plan changes in jumps; the slope is a guide). Two lessons: the best budget is where marginal ROI falls to the hurdle rate (here Rs 47, if profit is the only goal), and a deeper discount can lower profit (C4's 20% is worse than its 10%).

**Data needed.** Uplift or response-curve estimates per customer and offer depth, expected cost, margin and basket per customer, and every business rule written as a constraint. The estimates should come from randomised data, because optimisers amplify errors in observational estimates.

**How it is evaluated.** Not by prediction error. The decision-focused learning literature stresses that minimising prediction error and minimising decision error "do not in general coincide" ([DFL survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.13565v4)), and Meituan reports that prediction errors accumulate through the optimiser in two-stage pipelines ([Zhou et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.15728)). The right test is realised incremental profit by decile of predicted ROI on a randomised holdout, the cost-curve analogue of the Qini chart (analyst recommendation). Booking.com reports its multiple-choice online algorithm stayed within a 0.245% gap of the exact solution and beat greedy by 51% ([Booking.com](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.13298v1)).

**Industry examples.** Meituan: a four-week A/B test with +14.3% incremental orders and +13.6% GMV (authors' figures) ([Zhou et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.15728)). Booking.com: the dynamic group treated 47% of the population and achieved about 74% of the uplift of treating everybody, ending at ROI 0.24 ([Goldenberg et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.06293)). Uber's Tarot system: a machine-learning layer predicts cost and return, a budget pacer reconciles planned and actual spend (utilisation reportedly rose from about 68% to a projected 99.99%) and a solver allocates ([Uber](https://www.uber.com/blog/solving-multiple-knapsack/)). Alibaba: a unified framework validated in online A/B tests. Mercari: roughly 88,000 customers per coupon type, with a robust-optimisation model that beat baselines most when the budget was limited, evaluated offline only ([Uehara et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.12865v1)). For consumer packaged goods, vendor pages describe promotion optimisation goals but disclose no method, and dunnhumby's quoted gains (0.25-0.5% margin, 1-3% cash sales, "10x ROI") carry no stated basis and should not be quoted as benchmarks ([dunnhumby](https://www.dunnhumby.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/Promotions_Analytics.pdf)).

**Limitations.** The optimiser spends on customers with the highest *estimated* ROI, so it systematically selects those whose uplift was over-estimated (the winner's curse; analyst reasoning, consistent with the robust-optimisation result). Items near the cutoff are fragile: if C3's true 5% purchase probability were 0.34 rather than 0.38, its profit would be 0.34 x 250 - 0.30 x 300 = **-Rs 5** instead of +Rs 5. Other failure modes (analyst list, unsourced): response curves extrapolated beyond tested discount depths, ignored cannibalisation and pull-forward, observational confounding, and excluding loyal customers who may be economically right to skip but relationship-sensitive.

### Method 4: Contextual bandits and adaptive learning

**What it is.** A contextual bandit chooses one action (an offer) per decision using the customer's and situation's features (the context), observes only the result of the action it chose, and updates continuously. It sits between a static A/B test and full reinforcement learning, which also plans sequences. Li, Chu, Langford and Schapire framed Yahoo's news personalisation this way, with a 12.5% click lift over a context-free bandit on over 33 million events ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1003.0146)).

**Business question.** "Which offer works best for which kind of customer *right now*, and how do we find out without wasting too much money on testing?"

**How it works.** The ladder, in the analyst's framing: batch segmentation gives fixed groups the same offer; an A/B test splits traffic evenly, picks a winner and stops learning; a multi-armed bandit shifts traffic to the winner during the test; a contextual bandit shifts traffic to the best offer for each customer type or situation; reinforcement learning also plans how today's offer changes tomorrow's customer. Exploration means deliberately showing a not-yet-proven offer to learn; exploitation means showing the best known one; regret is the profit lost against always showing the best offer in hindsight. The common algorithms are epsilon-greedy (a fixed share, say 10%, get a random offer), upper-confidence-bound methods such as LinUCB (highest estimate plus an uncertainty bonus) and Thompson sampling (draw from each offer's belief and show the highest draw) ([Russo et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.02038)). In a Swiggy test LinUCB and Thompson sampling showed no significant difference ([Swiggy Bytes](https://bytes.swiggy.com/personalising-the-swiggy-homepage-layout-part-ii-450c55a40058)), so the choice matters less than the engineering around it.

**Worked example (invented numbers; arithmetic checked).** Three offers: A = 10% off, B = buy-one-get-one, C = free delivery. Each starts with a flat belief, Beta(1,1), about its redemption rate. Customer 1 arrives; draws are A 0.42, B 0.71, C 0.30, so B is shown. It is redeemed, so B becomes Beta(2,1). Customer 2 draws A 0.55, B 0.48, C 0.61, so C is shown; not redeemed, C becomes Beta(1,2). After 200 exposures each, suppose A has 30 redemptions, B 50 and C 20. The beliefs become Beta(31,171), Beta(51,151) and Beta(21,181), with means 31/202 = **15.3%**, 51/202 = **25.2%** and 21/202 = **10.4%**. B is now drawn most often, A occasionally, C rarely. The contextual version keeps separate beliefs per context, such as lapsed weekday app users and weekend family shoppers, so B may win in one and C in the other. Crucially these are *redemption* rates. If the reward is redemption, the bandit learns to give deep discounts to people who would have bought anyway. The reward should be incremental margin: margin on the basket minus discount given minus estimated baseline margin without an offer (analyst recommendation). Sourced practice is that marketers pick the key performance indicator, as at OfferFit/Braze ([Braze](https://www.braze.com/resources/articles/what-is-offerfit)), and Netflix deliberately used "quality play" rather than any play to avoid learning clickbait ([Netflix](https://netflixtechblog.com/artwork-personalization-c589f074ad76)).

**Data needed.** Logged decisions with the probability that each offer was shown (the propensity), context features and delayed outcomes such as redemptions days later. Without logged propensities, history cannot be reused to test new policies. Inverse propensity scoring shows the correction: an offer shown with probability 0.2 and redeemed counts as 5 virtual redemptions (1 / 0.2); one shown with probability 0.8 counts 1.25. Rare shown offers produce noisy weights, which doubly robust estimators reduce ([Dudik et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1103.4601)).

**How it is evaluated.** Offline replay and off-policy evaluation, then a live test. Netflix keeps logged impressions where the random assignment matched the new model's choice and computes the take fraction on that subset, using logged probabilities to correct skewed sampling; offline and online results correlated "reasonably well" ([Netflix](https://netflixtechblog.com/artwork-personalization-c589f074ad76)). The Open Bandit Dataset and Pipeline from ZOZOTOWN gives public logged data for comparing estimators ([Saito et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.07146)). After that, confirm with a live A/B test and ramp traffic gradually (for example 5%, 25%, 100%) with guardrails such as minimum margin, maximum discount depth and frequency caps (analyst recommendation, unsourced).

**Industry examples.** Netflix (artwork), Yahoo (news), Lyft (incentive budgets, UI and pricing parameters, no quantitative results published), Uber (allocation with planned closed-loop exploration), Swiggy (homepage widget ranking; 30 days of offline evaluation where top-1 order share beat baseline after about 15 days, no live A/B reported at publication) and Braze/OfferFit (vendor-reported results such as +14% reactivation for one sports streamer, unverified) ([Lyft](https://eng.lyft.com/a-review-of-multi-armed-bandits-applications-at-lyft-132550dd4df3); [Uber](https://www.uber.com/blog/solving-multiple-knapsack/); [Swiggy Bytes](https://bytes.swiggy.com/personalising-the-swiggy-homepage-layout-part-ii-450c55a40058); [Braze](https://www.braze.com/resources/articles/what-is-offerfit)). Swiggy is the only Indian company with a public bandit write-up in the notes, so do not claim Zomato, Flipkart or Reliance use bandits. Starbucks should be described as "reinforcement-learning-based personalised recommendations" using inventory, weather and time of day, not as an offer-level bandit ([Microsoft Source](https://news.microsoft.com/source/features/digital-transformation/starbucks-turns-to-technology-to-brew-up-a-more-personal-connection-with-its-customers/)).

**Limitations.** Productisation is hard: context design, monitoring, offline evaluation, adding new offers to a live system and imposing constraints ([Productization challenges](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.04884)). Exploration costs money; Netflix notes it suits cases where exploration is cheap ([Netflix](https://netflixtechblog.com/artwork-personalization-c589f074ad76)), and every exploratory discount costs margin, so exploration share should be capped. In-store redemption is slow and observed only for identified customers, so updates would be daily or weekly batches (analyst inference). A bandit is myopic, so fatigue and lifetime value need a proxy reward such as 30- or 90-day retention-weighted spend, or reinforcement learning. Plain conversion cannot see Sleeping Dogs, so include a "no offer" arm. Platform risk is real: **Microsoft stated that Azure AI Services Personalizer was to be retired on 1 October 2026** ([Azure Updates](https://azure.microsoft.com/updates/ai-services-personalizer-will-be-retired-on-1-october-2026)); the post-retirement position could not be confirmed, which argues for portable open-source tooling such as Vowpal Wabbit or the Open Bandit Pipeline.

## One pipeline: predict, measure, optimise, adapt, with a decision engine around it

The four methods are not alternatives. They form a chain in which each stage fixes a flaw of the previous one: predict (response and demand models), measure (uplift and holdouts replace response with effect), optimise (the knapsack turns effects into a budgeted plan), adapt (bandits keep the plan current) and re-measure (a persistent holdout checks everything). Booking.com's published pipeline is the closest sourced precedent: a randomised test gives treatment and control data, uplift variants are trained and ranked, customers are treated until the ROI constraint binds, offline evaluation uses Qini and AUUC, online validation follows in a multi-arm test, and the threshold is dynamically recalibrated as seasonality changes ([Goldenberg et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.06293)). The same source shows why the optimisation layer matters: classical uplift models did well on AUUC but "under-perform in ROI-related measures" because they ignore cost. The 2025 CMR paper gives a vivid parallel: one campaign had higher conversion (18.04%) yet lower profit per customer ($5.18) than the control ($5.26), while the uplift-optimised policy earned $5.46 at a lower 17.32% conversion ([CMR](https://cmr-mig.berkeley.edu/assets/documents/pdf/2025-11-to-treat-or-not-to-treat-five-lessons-learned-from-using-uplift-modeling-to-optimize-marketing-campaigns.pdf)). Conversion can rise while profit falls.

| Layer | Job | Inputs | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Data | Consolidated customer records with quality checks and lineage | Transactions, offer logs, prices, calendar | Point-in-time features |
| 2. Eligibility rules | Who may get what | Business rules, consent, supplier commitments | Candidate offers per customer |
| 3. Scoring | Propensity, timing, uplift, expected margin | Features, randomised test data | Scores per customer-offer pair |
| 4. Optimiser | Budget, frequency caps, minimum margin | Scores, costs, constraints | Allocation |
| 5. Exploration | Controlled learning, "no offer" arm | Logged propensities | Final offer per decision |
| 6. Delivery and logging | Channel, timing, outcome capture | Chosen offer | Logged exposure and result |
| 7. Measurement | Persistent holdout, drift and calibration monitoring | Logs | Incremental profit; retraining triggers |

This layout is the analyst's synthesis, loosely matched to the Uber Tarot flow and to McKinsey's description of next-best-experience: four components of data engineering, advanced analytics (propensity, channel and value models with a decision-orchestration layer applying business rules), generative AI for content and a campaign delivery platform ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/next-best-experience-how-ai-can-power-every-customer-interaction)). A practical maturity progression, again the analyst's framing, is rules by segment first, then propensity or value models ranking offers within eligibility, then a decision engine adding constraints.

**Contact policy and offer fatigue.** McKinsey recommends a single contact policy across billing, customer experience, marketing and sales; a European telecom stopped outbound campaigns to customers with open complaints, which raised satisfaction and improved cross-sell and churn. The stated outcomes (satisfaction +15 to 20%, revenue +5 to 8%, cost to serve -20 to 30%) have no year and the article's examples come from telecom, airlines and payments, not retail ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/next-best-experience-how-ai-can-power-every-customer-interaction)). A contact policy sets a maximum number of offers per customer per period, a cooling-off window and an explicit "no offer" option; it enters the knapsack as a constraint and the bandit as a context feature. Inc42 reports that Zepto cut blanket cashback after customers "came to expect an incentive on every order", with some splitting orders, which raised fleet cost without better unit economics, though the source relies on unnamed internal accounts ([Inc42](https://inc42.com/features/zeptos-retention-play-fewer-discounts-premium-carts-a-paid-club/)). No sourced figures were found for frequency caps or fatigue thresholds.

**What public retailers show.** Kroger delivered more than 6 million unique customised offers to Plus Card members through direct mail in Q1 2017 ([Grocery Dive](https://www.grocerydive.com/news/grocery--krogers-analytics-and-personalized-pricing-keep-it-a-step-ahead-of-its-comp/534926/)); Starbucks reported 34.3 million active US Rewards members in Q1 FY24, up 13%, with its CEO saying Deep Brew identifies member cohorts for relevant incentives ([Marketing Dive](https://www.marketingdive.com/news/starbucks-rewards-loyalty-q3-personalization/706322/)). These show personalised offer scale, not uplift or bandit methods. The Target pregnancy-prediction story is widely repeated but weakly substantiated: Eric Siegel calls the implication that the model identified a teenager "unsubstantiated" ([KDnuggets](https://www.kdnuggets.com/2014/05/target-predict-teen-pregnancy-inside-story.html)), so use it as a trust cautionary tale, not a proof of accuracy.

## Extra findings beyond the core methods

**Customer lifetime value should limit who is discounted.** High-value customers who already buy are Sure Things whose discounts are pure leakage; budget is better spent on lapsed or switching shoppers. The Anderson and Simester results (above) are the field evidence that deep discounts to established customers can lower future purchases, and the Kroger/84.51 test drew previously lapsed customers (not shopped in four weeks) rather than many new ones ([84.51](https://www.8451.com/knowledge-hub/technology/measuring-the-impact-of-a-new-promotion-84-51-helped-kroger-derive/)). A bandit with a short-term reward is myopic, so a lifetime-value proxy belongs in the reward (analyst recommendation). BG/NBD-type models give the expected-future-purchases input; no CLV benchmark was read.

**Elasticity ties depth to data.** DML with a continuous treatment gives an elasticity curve and causal forests give it per customer (analyst inference consistent with Chernozhukov et al., who name elasticities as a target ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.00060))); Didi's generalised causal forest estimates a non-parametric dose-response for continuous discounts ([Didi](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.10975)). The knapsack example shows why the profit-optimal depth is often shallower than the volume-optimal one.

**Cannibalisation, pull-forward and halo move the answer.** Overall effectiveness is promoted-product uplift minus cannibalisation plus halo, minus pull-forward ([Tredence](https://www.tredence.com/blog/decoding-the-metrics-a-deep-dive-into-calculating-promotion-effectiveness); that page's cross-elasticity worked example contains arithmetic and sign errors, so do not copy its numbers). Leeflang and colleagues found cross-category effects modest: complementary effects average about 20% and substitution about 11% of a promotion's own effect, a net spin-off of about 9% ([Aston](https://research.aston.ac.uk/en/publications/decomposing-the-sales-promotion-bump-accounting-for-cross-categor/)). An Aalto thesis relays that drops in other products' sales averaged about 30% of a promotion's volume uplift, and found that elastic-net cannibalisation estimates degraded with more products, with three years of data giving about 30% to 40% lower error than 1.5 years in simulations ([Aalto](https://sal.aalto.fi/publications/pdf-files/ther18_public.pdf)). For own-label the story is nuanced: switching from national brands is cannibalisation to the supplier but a margin-mix gain to the retailer (analyst inference).

**Trade promotion optimisation is the supplier-side counterpart.** CPG firms invest about 20% of revenue in trade promotions, a figure that appears second-hand via TELUS ([TELUS](https://www.telus.com/agcg/en-us/blog-resources/trade-promotion-roi)). The conceptual point is that retail and CPG TPO optimises product-week-mechanic combinations at store or chain level, while platforms optimise customer-level coupons (analyst inference). Vendor pages link promotions to inventory so offers match supply, but the page cited has internal inconsistencies ([SymphonyAI](https://www.symphonyai.com/glossary/promotion-optimization)).

**Personalised pricing and fairness draw regulatory attention.** In January 2025 the US Federal Trade Commission published preliminary findings from a 6(b) study of "surveillance pricing": intermediaries (Mastercard, Accenture, PROS, Bloomreach, Revionics, with McKinsey documents analysed) serving at least 250 clients including grocers and apparel retailers, using location, demographics, browsing, purchase history, mouse movements and abandoned carts; the study was ongoing and later outcomes were not checked ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/node/87270)). Press headlines indicate Instacart ended pricing experiments around 22 December 2025 after Consumer Reports and Groundwork reporting and an FTC inquiry, but only headlines were read, so treat the details as unverified ([Groundwork](https://groundworkcollaborative.org/news/facing-backlash-and-ftc-probe-instacart-ends-pricing-experiments-in-wake-of-groundwork-report/); [ADN](https://www.adn.com/business-economy/2025/12/22/instacart-halts-ai-testing-program-that-raised-costs-for-some-shoppers)). A CCI market study on AI and competition (reported 9 October 2025 by a legal blog; the release date was not given) says AI can set different prices per consumer profile, efficient but potentially harmful to vulnerable consumers through reduced price visibility or hidden profiling, and recommends self-audits, transparency and coordination with MeitY and the Data Protection Board ([IndiaLaw](https://www.indialaw.in/md/blog/competition-act/cci-report-examines-ais-impact-on-competition-law/)). Kroger says it does not personalise base prices, only loyalty discounts, with prior purchases the "most determinative factor"; Consumer Reports found a 62-page profile with many errors and experts warned personalised discounts could widen wealth gaps and that opting out may cost loyalty benefits ([Consumer Reports](https://www.consumerreports.org/money/questionable-business-practices/kroger-secret-grocery-shopper-profiles-unfair-a1011215563/)). The reading (analyst inference) is that a "personalised discount" can be read as a personalised price. No EU personalised-pricing rules were retrieved.

**India's DPDP Act constrains offer profiling, and its dates need checking.** The Digital Personal Data Protection Rules were notified in November 2025 with phased commencement. **Sources differ on the notification date: one set of sources says 13 November 2025 and another says Friday 14 November 2025, so check the Gazette.** The phases reported are 13 November 2025 (Board and definitions), 13 November 2026 (Rule 4 and the First Schedule on consent-manager registration) and 13 May 2027 (the remaining substantive obligations: notice, consent standards, security safeguards, breach intimation, data-principal rights, retention and erasure, Significant Data Fiduciaries, children) ([ComplianceHub](https://compliancehub.wiki/india-dpdp-consent-manager-november-2026-phase-two-deadline-compliance/), a secondary source; [JSA](https://www.jsalaw.com/?p=55573); [Hogan Lovells](https://www.hlc.com/en/publications/indias-digital-personal-data-protection-act-2023-brought-into-force-); [Deccan Herald](https://www.deccanherald.com/amp/story/india%2Fcentre-notifies-dpdp-rules-implementation-planned-in-phases-spread-over-12-18-months-3798465)). Notices must itemise data and purpose, withdrawal must be about as easy as consent, and under-18s need verifiable guardian consent ([JSA](https://www.jsalaw.com/?p=55573)); Hogan Lovells adds that large e-commerce platforms must delete data no later than three years after last interaction. **A possible compression of the 18-month window to 12 months (making the final date 13 November 2026) was reported by Mondaq as a MeitY proposal from January 2026, but another May 2026 source mentions no compression, and its status is unverified.** **Penalty figures also differ by source**: one reports up to Rs 250 crore per breach, another INR 2.5 billion (the same sum), and JSA quotes INR 200 crore for reporting failures; these must be checked against the Act's schedule ([India Briefing](https://india-briefing.com/news/india-dpdp-compliance-timeline-enforcement-2026-27-44740.html); [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/india/data-protection/1773554/meity-plans-to-cut-short-dpdp-compliance-timeline-and-notify-cross-border-restrictions-for-sdfs)). The practical implication (analyst reading, not legal advice) is that purpose-specific consent and easy withdrawal affect behaviour-based promotion targeting, and loyalty consent flows likely need review before the later of the dates; as of October 2026 the consent-manager phase is imminent.

**Explainability, drift and test governance.** Reason codes should be phrased as business reasons and complemented by interpretable models; a retail bandit review notes logistic-regression weights can be exposed for interpretability ([arXiv 2505.16918](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.16918v1)). Offline-trained models can be biased toward history and need recalibration for seasonality and long-term effects ([arXiv 2308.09066](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.09066)), and Booking.com calls dynamic threshold recalibration "common practice". Monitoring ideas (population stability index of scores, calibration by decile, realised versus predicted lift per wave) and test governance (pre-registered metric, sample-size calculation, persistent holdout) are the analyst's recommendations; no drift-monitoring source was retrieved. Fairness checks, such as not giving systematically worse offers by proxy for sensitive attributes, are recommended without a sourced norm.

**Public datasets and synthetic-data guidance.** For demos: the dunnhumby Complete Journey data (about 2,500 frequent-shopper households, two years of transactions, with some direct-marketing contact history; licence not stated on the page, only a link to terms) ([dunnhumby](https://dunnhumby.com/sourcefiles)); Criteo uplift data from randomised incrementality tests (11 dense features, conversion rate 0.00229, treatment ratio 0.846; advertising, not retail; the catalogue page is inconsistent on row count) ([TFDS](https://www.tensorflow.org/datasets/catalog/criteo)); the Open Bandit Dataset for off-policy evaluation ([zr-obp](https://github.com/st-tech/zr-obp)); the simulated Starbucks offers data ([TDS](https://towardsdatascience.com/starbucks-offer-dataset-udacity-capstone-7b562843ff47)); and Rossmann and M5 for promotion demand forecasting, whose licences were not checked. Simulated data has designed patterns, so accuracy on it should not be quoted as real-world performance; label any simulated ROI as illustrative and state that validation needs a real randomised holdout (analyst recommendation). Toolkits include BTYD and PyMC-Marketing for BG/NBD, scikit-learn for metrics and calibration, and pyuplift for Criteo ([BTYD](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/BTYD/html/BTYD-package.html); [PyMC-Marketing](https://pymc-marketing.readthedocs.io/en/latest/notebooks/clv/bg_nbd.html), existence from search only).

**Indian quick-commerce context.** Survey and press sources support deal-sensitive, quick-commerce-heavy shopping, but none quantifies feature importance. NielsenIQ's 2024 shopper survey found 40% of Indian modern-trade shoppers switch stores for better deals and that weekly main-grocery trips rose from 12% to 32% ([NIQ](https://nielseniq.com/global/en/insights/analysis/2024/five-key-trends-shaping-the-future-of-grocery-shopping-in-india)); a Cint survey reported by IT Supply Chain said 32% cite discounts and 36% fast delivery as main drivers of Diwali quick-commerce purchases, a vendor panel with undisclosed sample ([IT Supply Chain](https://itsupplychain.com/diwali-discounted-delivered-quick-commerce-set-to-disrupt-the-festival-of-lights/)); and an Empower India study reported by Business Today said 89% of UPI users consider rewards or cashback when choosing a payment app, sample not stated ([Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/personal-finance/news/story/89-of-upi-users-consider-rewards-before-choosing-a-payment-app-amazon-pay-tops-rewards-satisfaction-survey-544783-2026-07-23)). Because payment-app rewards compete with retailer offers, bank and UPI offers should be logged as separate exposure variables, and useful India-specific features include payment instrument, festival-window flags, order channel and basket mission (analyst inference). On discounting economics, Blinkit's chief executive said a strong quick-commerce business cannot rely on heavy discounting ([MediaNama](https://www.medianama.com/2026/01/223-strong-quick-commerce-business-heavy-discounting-blinkit-ceo/)); Zepto raised its free-delivery threshold from Rs 99 to Rs 149 and then Rs 199, and Bernstein estimates discounts fell from 18-20% of MRP at IPO filing to about 14-16% ([Inc42](https://inc42.com/features/zeptos-retention-play-fewer-discounts-premium-carts-a-paid-club/)); BigBasket's FY26 loss widened to Rs 3,073 crore on revenue of Rs 8,223 crore ([YourStory](https://yourstory.com/2026/07/bigbasket-losses-widen-as-revenues-grows-marginally)). Tata's NeuCoins (one rupee each, shared across Tata brands) come from a pre-launch 2022 report ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2022/02/17/tataneu/)). Reliance Retail's reported 378 million registered customers and quick commerce at about 20% of retail revenue come from a press report of an investor presentation and should be verified against the filing ([YourStory](https://yourstory.com/2026/01/reliance-scales-quick-commerce-to-16-million-daily-orders)). No public detail of Reliance, DMart or BigBasket personalisation methods was found, so do not assert how they operate internally. Common Indian mechanics (rupee-off on minimum spend, combos, bank offers, free-delivery thresholds, paid memberships) are practitioner knowledge, not sourced here.

Six gaps matter most. First, no verifiable public uplift case study from Tesco/dunnhumby, Kroger/84.51 (beyond the store test), Walmart, Instacart, Albert Heijn or any Indian retailer was found; grocers very likely use test-versus-control measurement, but that is inference. Second, price elasticity estimation, CLV benchmarks, multinomial logit and hierarchical Bayesian promotion models, sequence models and drift monitoring were not sourced. Third, the Blattberg and Neslin book, Kahneman and Tversky (1979) and Gedenk and Neslin (1999) could not be retrieved and are not cited. Fourth, the telecom and bank uplift cases rest on older papers. Fifth, no evidence was found on cross-sell promotions, BOGO versus multi-buy, thresholds or personalised versus mass offer effectiveness. Sixth, the Azure Personalizer post-retirement state and the FTC's outcome after January 2025 were not checked. Treat all figures marked invented, illustrative or analyst inference as teaching numbers and say so when presenting.

## Conclusion

The central shift is from prediction to intervention. Predicting that a customer will buy answers the wrong question, because the profitable question is whether the offer changes the purchase, and once that is accepted the rest follows in order: causal measurement makes the answer believable, budget optimisation turns a ranked list into a spend decision with a visible stopping point, and bandits keep the decision current. The prediction layer remains essential, but its job changes: calibrated propensities, baseline demand and timing become inputs to a decision, not the decision itself, and accuracy, the metric most people reach for first, is the one least able to tell a profitable model from a harmful one.

Two findings should shape how the story is told. The lifecycle evidence points the same way as the uplift logic: deeper discounts helped first-time customers' later purchases but hurt established customers', so even the "right" customer depends on lifecycle state and not on a segment label. And the honest limits are part of the message: feature-importance evidence is thin, loss aversion is contested for grocery, public grocer case studies of uplift or bandits are absent, and the regulatory ground in India and the United States is moving, so a manager who hears these limits stated up front is more likely to trust the methods that are well supported. The next concrete step is a randomised holdout, which turns a baseline estimate into a measurement and supplies honest inputs to every later stage.
